The Price of Looking Rich: When Shopping Becomes a Financial Investment in Nonsense

A fictional handbag, a very real financial problem, and the extraordinary ability of modern marketing to turn debt into a lifestyle.

MoneyTrackTemplates · Research-based editorial · October 2026

Madison's Magnificent Investment

Madison is 23, employed, ambitious and determined to build wealth. Unfortunately, her financial adviser is a woman on TikTok whose principal qualification is owning seventeen designer handbags.

One afternoon, Madison discovers an extraordinary financial opportunity: a $2,000 designer handbag.

According to her digital adviser, the handbag is an investment. Its timeless design, prestigious label and limited availability practically guarantee financial sophistication.

Madison is delighted. Finally, an investment she can carry to brunch.

There's just one inconvenience. Madison doesn't have $2,000.

Fortunately, she has a credit card.

Problem solved.

Within minutes, Madison has joined the distinguished ranks of people who believe purchasing something expensive is the same as acquiring an appreciating asset.

Her handbag arrives beautifully packaged.

Her credit card statement arrives somewhat less beautifully packaged.

The Mathematics of Magnificence

Let's examine Madison's investment portfolio, which currently consists of one handbag and an impressive amount of optimism.

Suppose she charges the $2,000 purchase to a credit card with a 24% annual percentage rate (APR). She makes monthly payments of $100, incurs no additional charges and makes every payment on time.

Under these simplified assumptions, she'll need approximately 26 months to eliminate the balance and pay roughly $580 in interest.

Her magnificent $2,000 handbag has cost approximately $2,580.

Two years later, Madison decides to sell it. Unfortunately, her particular handbag hasn't become a collector's treasure. Suppose a buyer offers her $900.

Madison accepts.

Her approximate financial loss: $1,680.

Naturally, not every luxury handbag depreciates. Some rare models appreciate, and some collectors earn substantial resale profits. Madison, however, purchased an ordinary retail product without researching its resale market, transaction costs or financing expenses.

She didn't conduct an investment analysis.

She watched a video.

Madison and her handbag are fictional. The borrowing calculation is illustrative, not a prediction of actual resale values or credit card terms. It assumes monthly interest of 2% and no other transactions.

When Four Easy Payments Become Twelve Financial Obligations

Madison's story may be fictional, but installment borrowing is widespread.

Buy now, pay later (BNPL) services allow customers to divide purchases into installments, frequently four payments and often without interest.

There are legitimate reasons to use these services. They can help consumers manage cash flow, particularly when an expense is necessary and repayment is affordable.

The difficulty begins when the affordability of each individual payment disguises the total amount a person has promised to repay.

A $200 purchase becomes four payments of $50.

Another $200 purchase becomes another four payments.

Add three more purchases, and suddenly the shopper has committed $1,000 without ever feeling as though they spent $1,000 in a single transaction.

The arithmetic remains stubbornly unimpressed by the marketing.

Research published by the Federal Reserve in May 2026 found that 16% of U.S. adults used BNPL during 2025. Among those users, 26% reported making at least one late payment. [1]

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's January 2025 research offers another perspective. Examining 2022 borrowing records from six major lenders, researchers found that approximately 63% of the BNPL borrowers studied originated multiple simultaneous loans at some point during the year. [2]

These findings do not establish that BNPL causes financial difficulties. They do illustrate why tracking outstanding commitments matters.

What Are People Financing?

The Federal Reserve's 2025 household survey provides an interesting look at the purchases Americans finance through BNPL services.

Clothing and accessories lead the categories, followed by electronics, furniture and appliances.

The results offer considerable material for our hypothetical Madison. Perhaps the handbag requires matching shoes. Perhaps the shoes require a new smartphone for photographing them.

But the data also reveal something considerably more serious.

One in five BNPL users reported financing groceries or food delivery.

Financing necessities because household income is insufficient is fundamentally different from financing a luxury purchase because someone on social media calls it an investment.

We shouldn't confuse the two.

The Credit Card Doesn't Care About Your Aesthetic

Credit cards provide convenience, purchase protections and, when managed carefully, useful financial flexibility.

However, interest changes the actual cost of purchases.

Suppose two consumers buy identical $2,000 handbags.

The first pays $2,000 without borrowing.

The second finances the purchase at 24% APR and repays $100 each month under Madison's simplified repayment assumptions.

Both receive identical handbags.

Only one receives roughly $580 in additional financing costs.

The second consumer has purchased precisely the same object at a substantially higher price.

The distinction isn't fashion.

It's mathematics.

The Investment Vocabulary Department

Modern marketing has developed a remarkable talent for borrowing terminology from finance.

A handbag becomes an investment piece.

A sneaker collection becomes an alternative asset portfolio.

An expensive vacation becomes an investment in yourself.

A new smartphone becomes an investment in productivity.

Occasionally, these descriptions have merit. A tool that helps someone earn income, training that improves professional skills, or a collectible purchased with careful market research may provide measurable benefits.

But calling something an investment doesn't automatically give it an investment return.

A purchase can be enjoyable, useful, beautiful or personally meaningful without being a financial investment.

The distinction becomes especially important when the purchase is financed.

Before accepting the investment label, a consumer might examine the acquisition cost, financing charges, maintenance expenses, resale market and realistic prospects for earning a return.

Unfortunately, that analysis is considerably less photogenic than an unboxing video.

The Invisible Price Tag

The greatest danger isn't necessarily one expensive purchase.

It's the accumulation of small financial commitments that gradually consume future income.

One installment plan finances the headphones.

Another finances the sneakers.

A credit card finances the handbag.

A subscription maintains access to an assortment of services that nobody remembers subscribing to.

Each individual payment appears manageable.

Collectively, they may leave little room for unexpected expenses, saving or reducing existing debt.

The Federal Reserve's findings about late payments and the CFPB's research on simultaneous loans demonstrate why the number and timing of obligations deserve attention.

Financial decisions don't occur independently. Every repayment competes with other uses of the same income.

Madison Discovers an Extraordinary New Investment

Six months after selling her handbag, Madison encounters another financial influencer.

This one recommends building an emergency fund, comparing borrowing costs and distinguishing assets from ordinary consumer purchases.

The influencer has fewer followers.

Her handbags are suspiciously ordinary.

Nevertheless, Madison investigates.

She discovers that money retained rather than spent can remain available for emergencies, future opportunities and purchases she genuinely values.

She also learns that financial independence doesn't require abandoning beautiful things.

It requires understanding what those things cost.

Madison still appreciates designer handbags.

She simply no longer expects them to finance her retirement.

And somewhere on TikTok, a woman with seventeen handbags is preparing an important announcement about investment-grade sunglasses.

The lesson: Borrowing always has a cost. Sometimes that cost is interest or fees. Even when a loan charges neither, its payments still commit money you could otherwise use in the future.
