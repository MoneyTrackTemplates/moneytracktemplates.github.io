Welcome to October

The Golden Month

October arrives beneath a changing sky, carrying bright leaves, cool mornings and the scent of woodsmoke. The garden offers its last abundance while the household turns toward warm suppers, earlier evenings and preparations for winter.

This is the month for gathering—apples, pumpkins, pantry provisions and a few good thoughts from the passing year. In these pages, we visit the garden, kitchen and October sky; meet animals preparing for colder days; explore old customs; and sit beneath the Hunter’s Moon.

There will also be household advice, savings ideas, horoscopes and a monthly checklist—with occasional supervision from Frida. Welcome to October, the golden month.

October at a Glance

October is a generous month, though it makes no promise to remain so. It offers bright afternoons, cool evenings, baskets of apples and leaves dressed better than most of us. Then, just as everyone grows comfortable, it sends a sharp wind beneath the door to see whether we have found the blankets. In 2026, October begins on a Thursday and finishes on a Saturday. It contains thirty-one days—ample time to prepare for winter, enjoy the harvest and misplace at least one rake beneath a pile of leaves.

What the Season Is Doing

Across much of the Northern Hemisphere, daylight shortens, nights grow cooler and plants begin withdrawing their strength toward roots, bulbs and seeds. Migrating birds follow ancient routes. Squirrels establish savings accounts composed chiefly of acorns. Gardeners watch the forecast. Households begin locating coats, checking heaters and wondering why three gloves remain but none of them match. October does not behave the same everywhere. Northern gardens may already meet frost while southern gardens continue producing peppers, herbs and tomatoes.

A Sensible Beginning

Before October gathers speed, choose three useful tasks: One task for the home — Test a smoke alarm, inspect the heater or wash the winter blankets. One task for the pantry — Use older food, preserve seasonal produce or plan several warming meals. One task for the purse — Review upcoming holiday expenses before November arrives with catalogs and persuasive lighting. A month need not be mastered all at once. The wise household prepares by degrees.

October Is

Calendars are crowded with commemorations, causes and cheerful declarations. Some deserve our attention; others chiefly remind us that nearly every object, vegetable and occupation has acquired its own special day. For Diana’s Country Almanac, we have chosen several October observances that invite useful action. Awareness is a good beginning, but it improves greatly when accompanied by a telephone call, an appointment, a safer password or a kindness performed before supper.

Breast Cancer Awareness Month

October encourages people to learn about breast health, family history and appropriate screening. Screening recommendations can differ according to age, medical history and individual risk, so personal decisions are best discussed with a qualified healthcare professional. One useful action: If you have postponed a medical appointment or an important conversation about family health history, place it on the calendar.

Domestic Violence Awareness Month

A safe home is more important than a tidy one. Domestic violence can affect people of every background, and assistance is available without judgment. The National Domestic Violence Hotline provides free, confidential support twenty-four hours a day. Call 800-799-SAFE (7233), text START to 88788, or use the website’s live chat. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. One useful action: Learn how to listen without blame. A calm, trustworthy person may become the first open door someone has seen in a long while.

National Disability Employment Awareness Month

National Disability Employment Awareness Month recognizes the skills and contributions of workers with disabilities and encourages workplaces to remove barriers that prevent good people from doing good work. One useful action: Examine whether a workplace, meeting, document or website is genuinely accessible—or merely believes itself to be. A Workplace Observation Talent is easily overlooked when the doorway was designed without everyone in mind.

Adopt a Shelter Dog Month

October is also a time to remember dogs waiting in shelters and rescue organizations. Adoption is a lasting responsibility, not a seasonal decoration. The right animal must suit the household’s time, space, finances and temperament. Those unable to adopt may still help by fostering, volunteering, donating supplies, sharing a reputable shelter’s listings or ensuring their own pets have identification and current veterinary care. The ASPCA offers guidance for people considering adoption. One useful action: Ask a local shelter what it actually needs. The answer may be less glamorous than expected—laundry detergent seldom poses for photographs—but far more helpful.

National Arts and Humanities Month

October also celebrates the arts and humanities: painting, music, literature, history, theater, dance and the long human habit of leaving evidence that we were here and had something to say. One need not purchase an expensive ticket to take part. Visit a library, attend a community performance, read a poem aloud, listen to unfamiliar music, interview an older relative or learn the history of a nearby building. One useful action: Preserve one family story in writing. Include names, places and the ordinary details that people assume they will never forget. A Household Truth A story remembered by one person is a treasure; a story written down becomes an inheritance.

Faith Tradition and the Turning Year

October has long been a month of preparation. Fields are cleared, provisions are counted and households begin looking toward winter. It is therefore no surprise that the month also carries days of prayer, remembrance and reflection. Christian observances differ among Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican and Protestant traditions, and not every church marks the same date in the same manner. The following are included as windows into history and custom—not as instructions for anyone’s private faith. A calendar may tell us when a day arrives. What people believe, remember and do with it is a much larger story.

October 2 — The Guardian Angels

In the Roman Catholic calendar, October 2 is the Memorial of the Holy Guardian Angels. The observance reflects the longstanding Christian belief that angels serve as heavenly messengers and guardians. Artists have frequently pictured guardian angels escorting children over bridges, along dangerous roads or past suspicious-looking cliffs. The children in such paintings seldom appear properly concerned, which may be why the angel was assigned in the first place. Whether regarded as doctrine, devotion or a beautiful symbol, the guardian angel represents a comforting idea: that no person travels entirely unnoticed. A thought for the day: Look after someone who may be carrying more than can be seen. October Wisdom We cannot all be angels, but most of us can answer a letter, carry a parcel or make a lonely person welcome.

October 4 — The Feast of Saint Francis of Assisi

October 4 is widely observed as the feast day of Saint Francis of Assisi, remembered for his religious devotion, voluntary poverty and enduring association with animals and the natural world. Many churches hold a Blessing of the Animals on or near his feast. Dogs arrive on leashes, cats arrive under protest and smaller creatures are conveyed in whatever arrangement their owners trust. The custom offers a fitting October reminder that animals depend upon human beings for more than affection. They require food, shelter, veterinary attention, safe surroundings and patient care when age or illness changes their needs. A thought for the day: Check an animal’s bedding, identification tag, food supply or coming cold-weather needs. A Barnyard Observation Kindness to animals is best measured after the photograph has been taken and the feeding pail still needs washing.

October 18 — The Feast of Saint Luke

Many Christian traditions commemorate Saint Luke the Evangelist on October 18. Traditionally associated with the Gospel of Luke and the Acts of the Apostles, he has also long been regarded as a physician and a patron of doctors, surgeons and artists. For that reason, the day can serve as a quiet occasion to remember those who care for the sick—and those who perform the less celebrated labor of scheduling appointments, changing linens, preparing meals, collecting prescriptions and sitting beside a bed when conversation has run out. A thought for the day: Thank someone whose work brings care, skill or comfort to others. A Household Truth Medicine may come in a bottle, but care generally arrives carrying a chair.

October 31 — All Hallows’ Eve

The evening before All Saints’ Day became known as All Hallows’ Eve, later shortened to Halloween. Over many centuries, Christian observance, local folk customs, seasonal traditions and commercial invention met one another and produced the holiday now familiar across much of the United States. Modern Halloween contains many layers. Church bells, prayers for the dead, carved lanterns, masquerades, neighborhood visits, ghost stories and bowls of individually wrapped candy did not all appear at once, nor did they begin in one place. History is rarely a single straight road. It is more often a village crossing where several roads meet, exchange hats and leave together after dark. For some Christians, October 31 remains the beginning of a period honoring saints and remembering the departed. For many other people, it is a community celebration of costumes, decorations and mild fright. A household may choose solemnity, festivity or an early bedtime with the porch light turned off. A thought for the evening: Enjoy the custom while protecting children, pets, visitors and anyone who must navigate a dark walkway.

October 31 — Reformation Day

Many Protestant Christians observe October 31 as Reformation Day, recalling the movement that reshaped Christianity and European history during the sixteenth century. The date is traditionally associated with Martin Luther’s Ninety-five Theses in 1517. The familiar account says that Luther posted them on the door of the Castle Church in Wittenberg. Historians continue to discuss the particulars of the posting, but the document itself and the controversy it helped ignite are not in doubt. The Reformation affected worship, politics, education, printing, language and the authority of both church and state. It also contributed to bitter conflict and violence. A thought for the day: Read a page of history from more than one point of view.

When Traditions Share a Date

October 31 demonstrates how several histories can occupy the same square on a calendar. One person may attend church. Another may study Reformation history. Another may escort a child dressed as a dinosaur from door to door. Some may do all three and consider the day properly used.

Historical Mystery: The Day Rome Left Important Business Alone

October 5 — Ancient Rome Evidence: Recorded in surviving Roman literature, though the exact meaning of the rite remains debated. On certain days of the Roman year, including October 5, an underground sacred place called the mundus was ceremonially opened. The announcement was mundus patet—“the mundus is open.” Ancient writers connected the place with the powers beneath the earth and the spirits of the dead. What the structure looked like, precisely where it stood and how all its rites were performed remain subjects of scholarly debate. What survives more clearly is the Romans’ caution. Macrobius, writing in late antiquity and drawing upon earlier authorities, reported that when the mundus stood open, Romans avoided beginning certain serious undertakings. Military action, troop movements, voyages and marriages were among the affairs considered ill-timed. Diana’s Little Wisdom A closed door sometimes protects us. An open one sometimes advises us to remain home. The Romans postponed important affairs when the underworld stood open.

This Month in History

October has carried automobiles onto the road, satellites into orbit and messages across a continent. It has also brought fire, daring, celebration and the final curtain of a celebrated magician. History rarely announces which ordinary-looking morning will later receive a chapter of its own. People wake, dress, complain about the weather and begin the day. By supper, the world may have acquired a new machine, a new hero or an entirely new set of problems.

October 1, 1908 — The Model T Goes on Sale

On October 1, 1908, Ford Motor Company began selling the Model T. Its production record survives in The Henry Ford’s collection. More than fifteen million Model Ts were produced before manufacturing ended in 1927. The automobile promised independence, but soon required roads, fuel, repairs, traffic rules, parking spaces and a firm opinion about how everyone else ought to drive. A Motoring Truth Every invention that saves time eventually gives us somewhere farther away to be.

October 4, 1957 — A Beeping Ball Changes the World

On October 4, 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite to orbit Earth. Sputnik was roughly the size of a beach ball and weighed about 184 pounds. It circled Earth in approximately 98 minutes, sending out radio signals that could be detected below. The satellite carried no crew and delivered no speech. It merely beeped—and governments listened with great concern. Its launch startled the United States, intensified the Cold War competition in science and technology and helped begin the Space Age. It also encouraged greater American investment in science education, aerospace research and the new federal agency that became NASA. NASA’s history of Sputnik describes how one modest-looking sphere produced consequences far larger than itself. A Skyward Observation Never underestimate a small object that arrives first and refuses to stop beeping.

October 8–10, 1871 — The Great Chicago Fire

On the evening of October 8, 1871, a fire began on Chicago’s West Side and spread rapidly through a city crowded with wooden buildings, wooden sidewalks and dry materials. Strong winds carried flames and burning debris across neighborhoods. The fire continued into October 10, destroying a vast portion of the city, killing hundreds and leaving roughly 100,000 people without homes. Rain late on October 9 helped, but rain alone did not provide a convenient ending to so large a catastrophe. For generations, a popular story blamed Mrs. O’Leary’s cow for kicking over a lantern. The tale became famous; the evidence did not. The exact cause of the fire remains uncertain. That distinction matters. A memorable story is not necessarily a documented one, particularly when it places convenient blame upon an immigrant woman and a cow unable to retain legal counsel. The disaster changed building practices, fire prevention and the physical future of Chicago. It also reminds us that cities, like households, often improve their safeguards only after learning why those safeguards were needed. Lore—or Likely? The cow started the fire: Famous folklore. The true cause: Undetermined. A Household Rule Check the stove, the chimney and the smoke alarms before trusting luck with the night watch.

October 14, 1947 — Faster Than Sound

On October 14, 1947, U.S. Air Force test pilot Charles “Chuck” Yeager flew the rocket-powered Bell X-1 faster than the speed of sound over California’s Mojave Desert. The aircraft, painted orange and named Glamorous Glennis for Yeager’s wife, was carried aloft beneath a larger airplane before being released. Yeager then fired its rocket engines and exceeded Mach 1. The achievement was remarkable enough without decoration, but the circumstances supplied some anyway. Two nights before the flight, Yeager broke two ribs in a horseback-riding accident. Concerned that he might be removed from the mission, he kept the injury largely quiet. A fellow pilot helped devise a short handle that allowed him to close the aircraft’s hatch despite his pain. A history of the flight is available from the American Physical Society. Courage is valuable, although an almanac must observe that concealing broken ribs before operating a rocket aircraft is not sound household policy. Please Do Not Try This at Home Determination is admirable. Medical attention also has much to recommend it.

October 24, 1861 — News Crosses the Continent

On October 24, 1861, the first transcontinental telegraph system was completed, connecting existing eastern and western networks through Salt Lake City. Before the telegraph, a message traveling across the United States depended upon ships, stagecoaches, railroads, riders and weather. The Pony Express had carried mail with impressive speed, but horses could not compete with electricity. Within days of the telegraph line’s completion, the Pony Express ceased operation. The Library of Congress records the occasion and its significance. The telegraph did not make communication instantaneous by modern standards. Messages had to be delivered to an office, transmitted in code, received and carried to their destination. Yet the country had crossed an astonishing boundary: information could travel faster than any person, horse or train carrying it. A Communications Lesson We spent centuries making messages travel faster. We might profitably spend an afternoon deciding which ones need to be sent.

October 28, 1886 — Liberty in the Harbor

On October 28, 1886, the Statue of Liberty was formally dedicated in New York Harbor. Its French name, La Liberté éclairant le monde, means Liberty Enlightening the World. Sculptor Frédéric-Auguste Bartholdi designed the statue, while engineer Gustave Eiffel and his company helped create its internal structure. France supplied the statue, and American fundraising supported the pedestal. The dedication occurred amid rain, fog, speeches, ships and enormous public interest. The statue would later become deeply associated with immigration, particularly because of its position near the route taken by millions of arriving immigrants. A less frequently noticed detail lies at Liberty’s feet: broken shackles and chains, symbolizing freedom from oppression and the abolition of slavery. The National Park Service provides additional facts about the monument and its design. Emma Lazarus wrote “The New Colossus” in 1883 to help raise money for the pedestal. The poem was not part of the original French design, but its words later became inseparable from the statue’s American meaning. A Civic Thought A monument is built of metal and stone. Its meaning is built—and tested—by the generations that inherit it.

In the October Garden

October gives the gardener two assignments: gather what the year has produced and prepare the ground for what has not yet begun. In colder regions, frost waits near the gate. In warmer regions, the garden may be entering its most comfortable season. One gardener is digging potatoes in a wool coat while another is planting lettuce in shirtsleeves. Both are following October correctly. The wise gardener consults the soil, the weather and the local forecast before consulting a national article—including this one. Garden Wisdom A calendar is useful, but a tomato has never learned to read one.

First, Find Your Frost Date

An average first-frost date is not an appointment made by the weather. It is a historical estimate based on past conditions. Frost may arrive early, late or in one low corner of the garden while leaving a sheltered bed untouched. Begin by learning three things: Your approximate first-frost and first-freeze dates Your USDA Plant Hardiness Zone The advice of your local Cooperative Extension office These are related, but they are not interchangeable. The USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map is based on a location’s average annual extreme minimum winter temperature. It helps gardeners judge which perennial plants are likely to survive the winter. It does not, by itself, tell you when the first autumn frost will occur or when vegetables should be planted. For local planting calendars, frost information and crop recommendations, consult a nearby land-grant university or Cooperative Extension service. The USDA maintains a directory of land-grant universities. Averages guide the gardener. Tonight’s forecast protects the basil.

Know the Difference: Frost and Freeze

A frost may form when surfaces cool enough for ice crystals to develop, sometimes even when the official air temperature remains slightly above freezing. Tender plants can be injured while sturdier crops remain unharmed. A freeze generally refers to air temperatures at or below 32°F. The length and severity of the cold matter. A brief dip is not the same as several hours of deep freezing. When cold threatens: Harvest tender vegetables and herbs that are ready. Water dry soil earlier in the day; moist soil generally holds heat better than dry soil. Cover low plants before sunset with frost cloth, sheets or lightweight blankets. Support heavier covers so they do not crush the plants. Remove or open coverings after temperatures rise the next morning. Move small containers to shelter when practical. Plastic can trap some warmth, but it should not rest directly against tender leaves. A covering that saves the plant but freezes to its foliage has misunderstood its employment. A Frosty Truth The gardener who waits until midnight to find the old bedsheet will discover that every useful sheet is presently on a bed.

Northern and Cold-Winter Gardens

In regions where winter arrives early, October is chiefly a month of harvesting, protecting and putting things in order.

Gather What Cannot Wait

Before a hard freeze: Harvest tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, cucumbers and tender herbs. Bring sound green tomatoes indoors to ripen gradually. Lift winter squash and pumpkins once mature, leaving a short stem attached. Dig potatoes carefully and keep damaged ones separate for early use. Collect dry beans only after the pods have dried sufficiently. Cut remaining flowers for one final indoor bouquet. Kale, collards, Brussels sprouts and some root crops can tolerate light cold, and their flavor may improve after cool weather. Their actual hardiness depends upon the variety, growing conditions and severity of the freeze.

Prepare for Winter

Drain and store hoses before repeated freezing weather. Empty fragile pots that may crack when wet soil freezes and expands. Clean soil from tools and dry them before storage. Mark the location of perennials before their tops disappear. Protect young trees from deer, rabbits and rodents where these are persistent problems. Apply mulch after the ground begins cooling, following recommendations for the particular plant and region. Mulch is meant to moderate soil-temperature swings, not to keep a northern garden warm enough for a January picnic.

Planting Bulbs

October is often suitable for planting spring-flowering bulbs such as daffodils, crocuses and tulips, provided the soil is cool but still workable. Choose varieties suited to the local climate, use the planting depth recommended for the bulb and avoid poorly drained locations. Where winters are mild, some bulbs may require chilling or may perform differently.

Mild-Winter and Southern Gardens

In many southern and mild-winter regions, October is not the garden’s farewell. It is the beginning of a second act. As summer heat eases, gardeners may be able to plant cool-season crops such as: Lettuce Spinach Arugula Radishes Carrots Beets Peas Broccoli Cabbage Kale Collards Onions or shallots suited to the region Cilantro and parsley The correct planting window varies greatly. Coastal areas, inland valleys, humid subtropical regions and high-elevation southern gardens may share a state while sharing very little weather. Check local recommendations before planting.

Western and Dry-Climate Gardens

In dry regions, cooler weather does not always mean the soil has received water. Reduce irrigation as temperatures fall and plant growth slows, but do not abandon newly planted trees, shrubs or vegetables to dry soil. Water according to actual conditions, plant needs, rainfall and local restrictions. Deep, well-spaced watering is generally more useful than frequent sprinkles that wet the surface and encourage shallow roots. Autumn can be an excellent planting season for climate-appropriate trees, shrubs and native perennials because cooler air reduces stress while roots begin establishing. Timing should follow local rainfall patterns and extension advice.

Garden Work Where Wildfire Is a Concern

Fallen leaves and dry stems can benefit soil and wildlife in appropriate garden areas. They should not, however, be allowed to gather against a house, beneath a deck, upon a roof or inside gutters in wildfire-prone country. Remove dead vegetation and combustible debris near structures. Clear roofs, gutters, decks and exterior stairways. Store firewood away from buildings according to local guidance. Prune dead branches and maintain access routes.

The October Vegetable Garden

Wherever October finds you, walk through the garden with three baskets—or three imaginary ones.

Basket One: Eat Soon

Place damaged produce, split tomatoes, bruised apples and imperfect vegetables here. Trim and use them promptly if they remain wholesome. A blemished vegetable may still make an excellent soup. It has simply lost the beauty contest and become available for honest work.

Basket Two: Store or Preserve

Choose sound, mature produce for storage, freezing, drying or another tested preservation method. Different crops require different storage conditions. Do not assume that every root, squash and apple desires the same cellar arrangement. For home canning, use a current, scientifically tested recipe from the National Center for Home Food Preservation or a Cooperative Extension service. Old family recipes may be treasured for flavor and memory, but canning safety must follow current tested methods.

Basket Three: Compost—or Discard

Healthy plant material may be composted. Plants showing serious disease, persistent pests or troublesome weed seeds may need different disposal according to local recommendations. A compost pile is a place for useful decay. It should not become a boardinghouse in which every garden problem spends the winter.

Flowers, Herbs and Perennials

October is a fine month to observe which plants earned their ground.

Save Seeds Carefully

Collect mature, dry seeds from healthy open-pollinated plants. Label each envelope with: Plant name and variety Color, if relevant Collection date Garden location Any useful observation Hybrid plants may not produce offspring identical to the parent. This is not deceit on the plant’s part; it is genetics declining to consult the catalog photograph. Dry seeds thoroughly before storing them in a cool, dry place.

Herbs

Before frost damages tender herbs: Cut healthy basil for pesto, freezing or drying. Harvest rosemary, thyme, sage and oregano as needed without stripping young plants bare. Bring suitable potted herbs indoors after inspecting them carefully for insects. Label frozen herbs unless you enjoy identifying green cubes by surprise in February.

Perennials

Remove material that is diseased, slimy or likely to cause a problem. Healthy seed heads and hollow stems may be left standing where practical. They can provide winter interest, food and shelter for birds and insects. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service encourages the creation of pollinator habitat, while the Xerces Society recommends providing nesting sites and shelter throughout the insects’ life cycles. A perfectly bare garden may please the eye of the gardener while disappointing every creature that expected to spend January there.

One Small Experiment

Plant a Patch of Garlic

Garlic is a patient crop. One clove is planted in autumn, waits through winter and grows into a bulb containing many cloves the following year. It is compound interest with roots. Fall planting is common in cold-winter regions, while timing and suitable varieties differ in warmer climates. The University of Minnesota Extension’s garlic guide recommends planting healthy cloves from a reputable seed source rather than ordinary grocery-store garlic, which may be poorly adapted or carry disease.

The Experiment

Obtain a garlic variety recommended for your climate. Choose a sunny location with well-drained soil. Separate the bulb into individual cloves shortly before planting. Keep the papery covering intact. Plant each clove with the pointed end upward. Follow local guidance for planting depth, spacing and mulch. Mark the row clearly. Write the variety and planting date in the garden ledger.

Lore—or Likely?

Do Thick Onion Skins Predict a Hard Winter?

The saying: Thick onion skins mean a severe winter is coming. The verdict: Charming lore, not a dependable forecast. The thickness and number of an onion’s outer layers are influenced by variety, growing conditions, maturity, curing and storage. The onion is responding to the season it has already experienced—not issuing a meteorological report for months it has not seen. Woolly caterpillars, persimmon seeds, squirrel tails and unusually busy spiders have also been asked to forecast winter. Such observations are enjoyable parts of seasonal folklore, but they should not replace official weather information or practical preparation. A Weather Rule Consult the onion for soup. Consult the forecast for snow.

Pumpkins Apples and the Autumn Harvest

October’s harvest arrives with excellent publicity. The pumpkin has secured porches, pies and nearly every scented candle in the country, while the apple has obtained orchards, school lunches and a permanent place in proverbs. Both are useful, handsome and capable of lasting well beyond October—provided no one bruises them, forgets them or attempts to turn every specimen into a centerpiece. Harvest Wisdom: The finest crop is not the one most admired in the basket, but the one still nourishing the household in winter.

The Honest Pumpkin

Pumpkins and other squashes are native to the Americas and were cultivated by Indigenous peoples for thousands of years before Europeans arrived. In many Indigenous agricultural traditions, squash grew alongside corn and beans in arrangements often called the Three Sisters, although methods varied among nations and climates. European settlers adopted pumpkins for soups, stews, breads, puddings and pies long before the pumpkin acquired its modern occupation as a porch ornament. The pumpkin has always been practical; we are the ones who gave it teeth.

Pumpkin or Winter Squash?

The distinction between a pumpkin and a winter squash is often culinary rather than strictly botanical. Large carving pumpkins are usually selected for size, shape and a hollow interior, while smaller pie pumpkins and dense winter squashes such as butternut, kabocha and Hubbard generally offer better flavor and texture for cooking. Decorative gourds should remain decorative unless the variety is known to be edible.

Choosing and Storing Squash

Choose pumpkins and winter squashes that are mature, firm, heavy for their size and free from soft spots, mold or serious damage. Carry them from underneath rather than by the stem, since a broken stem creates an opening for decay. Store only sound, mature squash in a cool, dry, ventilated location and inspect it regularly, using damaged but wholesome specimens first.

Choosing Apples by Use

Sweet, crisp apples such as Gala, Fuji and Honeycrisp are popular for fresh eating, while firmer varieties such as Granny Smith, Braeburn, Jonagold and Pink Lady often perform well in baking. Softer apples, including McIntosh and Cortland, can cook down nicely for sauce, and firm late-season varieties generally keep longer in storage.

Caring for the Apple Harvest

Handle apples gently, use bruised fruit first and store only sound specimens in cool conditions without allowing them to freeze. Apples naturally release ethylene, a plant hormone involved in ripening, so good ventilation and regular inspection help extend their storage life.

Lore—or Likely? One Bad Apple Spoils the Barrel

This old saying has a sound basis. A damaged or decaying apple may spread mold and microorganisms through direct contact, while increased ethylene can hasten changes in nearby fruit. One imperfect apple does not condemn the whole barrel, but one neglected apple can certainly create additional trouble.

From Harvest to Pantry

Pumpkins and apples may be eaten fresh, shared, stored, frozen, dried or preserved with a current, tested method. The method should suit both the food and the household, because there is no thrift in preserving twelve jars of something nobody will eat. Bruised but wholesome apples can become sauce, sound squash can be roasted and frozen, clean pumpkin seeds can be toasted, and extra produce can be shared before its condition declines.

A Note About Pumpkin Safety

Pumpkin and winter squash are low-acid foods, so home canning requires tested instructions. The National Center for Home Food Preservation provides a pressure-canning method for properly prepared cubes of pumpkin or winter squash but does not recommend home canning purée, mashed pumpkin or pumpkin butter because safe heat penetration cannot be assured. Cooked purée can instead be frozen in measured portions. Tradition may choose the spice, but science must choose the preservation method.

A Curious History: How Pumpkin Pie Became Pumpkin Pie

Modern pumpkin pie did not appear fully formed at an early colonial feast wearing whipped cream. Pumpkins were available, but familiar stories about colonists filling hollow pumpkins with milk and baking them in ashes are more picturesque than firmly documented. By 1796, Amelia Simmons’s American Cookery included “pompkin” puddings made with pumpkin, milk or cream, eggs, molasses and spices baked in pastry. The New York State Library provides access to an early edition. Today’s pie is the result of Indigenous crops, European pastry traditions, American cookbooks, changing ovens, canned purée and highly successful marketing.

From the Old Almanac Shelf

The eighteenth-century cook began with a pumpkin and a fire. The twentieth-century cook opened a can and consulted the label. The twenty-first-century cook may do either one, though neither may begin eating until someone has photographed the result.

Frida Inspects the Harvest

Frida, keeper of the household ledger and guardian of the pantry, judges an abundant harvest by what is actually used. Dianas Country Kitchen

October cooking should warm the room, use what the season provides and leave enough for tomorrow’s lunch. These recipes were created for the Almanac with ordinary ingredients, exact measurements and no instruction to add “a sufficient quantity” of anything.

Golden Pumpkin and Apple Soup

Pumpkin and apple make a smooth autumn soup that is lightly sweet but firmly belongs at the supper table. Serve it with toasted bread, a sandwich or a small salad. Makes: 6 servings Preparation: 15 minutes Cooking time: About 30 minutes

Ingredients

• 2 tablespoons butter or olive oil

• 1 medium yellow onion, chopped

• 1 medium tart apple, peeled, cored and chopped

• 2 garlic cloves, minced

• 1 teaspoon dried thyme

• ½ teaspoon dried sage

• ¼ teaspoon ground nutmeg

• 1 can (15 ounces) plain pumpkin purée

• 3 cups chicken or vegetable broth

• 1 cup whole milk or half-and-half

• ½ teaspoon salt, plus more to taste

• ¼ teaspoon black pepper

Instructions

1. Melt the butter in a large saucepan over medium heat. Add the onion and apple and cook for 7 to 8 minutes, stirring occasionally, until softened.

2. Add the garlic, thyme, sage and nutmeg. Cook for 30 seconds.

3. Stir in the pumpkin, broth, salt and pepper. Bring to a gentle boil, reduce the heat and simmer uncovered for 15 minutes.

4. Blend the soup carefully with an immersion blender until smooth. If using a countertop blender, work in small batches and allow steam to escape safely.

5. Stir in the milk and warm gently for 3 to 5 minutes without boiling. Taste and adjust the seasoning.

6. Storage: Refrigerate promptly in a covered container and use within 3 to 4 days.

7. Frida’s Thrifty Kitchen Note: Freeze leftover pumpkin purée in a labeled container rather than allowing half a can to begin a new civilization in the refrigerator.

Old-Fashioned Chicken and Dumplings

This is a thick, comforting chicken stew topped with tender drop dumplings. The recipe uses one pot, although it will still somehow produce several spoons. Makes: 6 servings Preparation: 25 minutes Cooking time: About 45 minutes

For the Chicken Stew

• 2 tablespoons butter or vegetable oil

• 1½ pounds boneless, skinless chicken thighs, cut into 1-inch pieces

• 1 teaspoon salt, divided

• ½ teaspoon black pepper, divided

• 1 medium yellow onion, chopped

• 3 medium carrots, sliced

• 2 celery stalks, sliced

• ¼ cup all-purpose flour

• 6 cups low-sodium chicken broth

• 1 teaspoon dried thyme

• ½ teaspoon dried sage

• 1 cup frozen peas

For the Dumplings

• 2 cups all-purpose flour

• 1 tablespoon baking powder

• 1 teaspoon salt

• 4 tablespoons butter, melted

• ¾ cup milk

Instructions

1. Heat the butter in a large Dutch oven or heavy pot over medium heat. Season the chicken with ½ teaspoon salt and ¼ teaspoon pepper. Brown it for 5 to 6 minutes, stirring occasionally. It need not be fully cooked yet. Transfer it to a plate.

2. Add the onion, carrots and celery to the pot. Cook for 6 minutes, stirring occasionally.

3. Sprinkle the ¼ cup flour over the vegetables and stir for 1 minute. Slowly add the broth while stirring to prevent lumps.

4. Return the chicken to the pot. Add the thyme, sage, remaining salt and remaining pepper. Bring to a gentle boil, reduce the heat and simmer for 12 to 15 minutes.

5. Meanwhile, whisk the dumpling flour, baking powder and salt in a bowl. Stir in the melted butter and milk just until a soft dough forms. Do not overmix.

6. Stir the peas into the stew. Drop the dumpling dough by rounded tablespoonfuls over the simmering surface, making about 12 dumplings.

7. Cover the pot tightly, reduce the heat to low and cook for 15 minutes without lifting the lid. Cut open one dumpling to confirm that its center is cooked and no longer doughy.

8. Check that the chicken has reached 165°F before serving.

9. Storage: Refrigerate within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days. The dumplings will soften but remain perfectly willing to be eaten.

10. Frida’s Thrifty Kitchen Note: Leftover roasted chicken may replace the raw chicken. Add 3 cups of chopped cooked chicken after the broth has thickened, then proceed with the dumplings.

Harvest Skillet with Sausage, Apples and Cabbage

Cabbage is economical, apples bring gentle sweetness and smoked sausage supplies enough flavor to make the entire skillet appear more expensive than it was. Makes: 4 to 6 servings Preparation: 15 minutes Cooking time: About 25 minutes

Ingredients

• 1 tablespoon vegetable or olive oil

• 14 ounces fully cooked smoked sausage, sliced

• 1 medium yellow onion, thinly sliced

• 6 cups thinly sliced green cabbage

• 2 firm apples, cored and sliced

• ½ cup chicken broth or water

• 2 tablespoons apple-cider vinegar

• 1 tablespoon Dijon mustard

• 1 teaspoon brown sugar, optional

• ½ teaspoon salt, or to taste

• ¼ teaspoon black pepper

Instructions

1. Heat the oil in a large skillet over medium heat. Add the sausage and cook for 4 to 5 minutes, turning occasionally, until lightly browned. Transfer it to a plate.

2. Add the onion and cook for 3 minutes. Add the cabbage, apples, broth, salt and pepper.

3. Cover and cook for 8 minutes, stirring once or twice, until the cabbage begins to soften.

4. Whisk the vinegar, mustard and brown sugar together. Add the mixture and the sausage to the skillet.

5. Cook uncovered for another 5 to 7 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the cabbage is tender and the liquid has mostly reduced.

6. Storage: Refrigerate promptly and use within 3 to 4 days. Reheat thoroughly before serving.

7. Frida’s Thrifty Kitchen Note: Half a cabbage may look modest at the market, but once sliced it reveals plans to feed the entire street. Use the remainder in soup, slaw or fried rice.

Simple Apple Brown Betty

Apple Brown Betty is an old-fashioned baked dessert made with apples and buttered bread crumbs rather than pastry. It is particularly useful when both the apples and the bread have passed their most photogenic days but remain wholesome. Makes: 8 servings Preparation: 20 minutes Baking time: 45 minutes Ingredients 6 cups peeled, cored and thinly sliced apples, about 2 pounds 1 tablespoon lemon juice ⅓ cup packed brown sugar 1 teaspoon ground cinnamon ¼ teaspoon ground nutmeg ¼ teaspoon salt 3 cups coarse fresh bread crumbs, about 6 slices of bread 6 tablespoons butter, melted ¼ cup apple cider or water

Instructions

1. Heat the oven to 350°F. Butter an 8-inch square baking dish.

2. Toss the apples with the lemon juice, brown sugar, cinnamon, nutmeg and salt.

3. Place the bread crumbs in another bowl and stir in the melted butter until evenly coated.

4. Spread one-third of the crumbs in the baking dish. Add half the apples, another third of the crumbs, the remaining apples and the remaining crumbs.

5. Pour the cider or water evenly around the edges.

6. Cover with foil and bake for 30 minutes. Remove the foil and bake for 15 minutes more, until the apples are tender and the top is golden.

7. Let the dessert rest for 10 minutes before serving.

8. Serve warm by itself or with a little cream, whipped cream or vanilla ice cream.

9. Storage: Cover and refrigerate within 2 hours. Use within 3 to 4 days.

10. Frida’s Thrifty Kitchen Note: Keep a bag of bread ends in the freezer. When the bag is full, the household has acquired the beginnings of stuffing, bread pudding, crumbs—or dessert.

When a Recipe Was Barely a Recipe

Older cookbooks often called recipes “receipts” and assumed the reader already possessed considerable kitchen knowledge. Directions might specify ingredients without exact quantities, request a “quick oven” without naming a temperature or advise the cook to bake something until it was done—a conclusion that remains accurate but unhelpful. Amelia Simmons’s 1796 American Cookery, available through the New York State Library, offers a fascinating look at this earlier style. Experienced cooks understood their fires, utensils and ingredients, but modern measurements allow a beginner to enter the kitchen without first serving a seven-year apprenticeship beside the hearth. Diana’s Kitchen Wisdom: A pinch may belong to tradition. A teaspoon is kinder to the reader.

A Note on Kitchen Safety

Cook poultry to a minimum internal temperature of 165°F, refrigerate perishable leftovers within 2 hours, and generally use refrigerated cooked leftovers within 3 to 4 days. The federal FoodSafety.gov temperature chart provides current guidance. When food has been left out too long or its safety is uncertain, thrift has reached the end of its authority.

Household Preparations

Before the Cold Comes Knocking

October is the proper month for discovering small household problems while they are still small. A loose window latch in October is an inconvenience; the same latch during a January storm becomes a personal enemy. Early preparation need not be expensive or elaborate. It begins with looking carefully at what the household already owns, what requires repair and what winter will make harder to ignore. Household Wisdom: Winter seldom creates every problem it brings to light. It merely removes the leaves that were hiding them.

Heating Without Surprises

Arrange professional service for furnaces, boilers and other fuel-burning heating equipment according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and have chimneys inspected or cleaned when needed. Never heat a home with a gas oven, charcoal grill or outdoor cooking appliance. A heating system should provide warmth without adding smoke, flame or mysterious odors to the household atmosphere.

Space Heaters

Place portable heaters on a stable surface at least three feet from bedding, curtains, upholstered furniture, paper and other combustible material. Plug them directly into a wall outlet rather than an extension cord or power strip, and turn them off before leaving the room or going to sleep. Keep children and pets away from them. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission provides additional home-heating and fire-safety guidance.

Smoke and Carbon Monoxide Alarms

Test smoke alarms monthly and replace them according to the manufacturer’s instructions; the U.S. Fire Administration advises replacing smoke alarms when they reach ten years of age. Install alarms in the locations recommended for the home, including inside and outside sleeping areas and on every level. Carbon monoxide alarms with battery power or battery backup should be placed near sleeping areas and maintained according to their instructions.

The Invisible Danger

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless and can cause sudden illness or death. Fuel-burning furnaces, fireplaces, generators, grills, engines and other equipment may produce it. Common symptoms include headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, chest pain and confusion. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds or poisoning is suspected, move everyone—including pets—to fresh air and contact emergency services. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention provides prevention and emergency information. A Serious Rule: If an alarm sounds, investigate the danger—not the inconvenience.

Pipes and Outdoor Faucets

Before freezing weather, disconnect garden hoses, drain or protect outdoor faucets as appropriate and inspect exposed pipes in unheated spaces. Know where the main water shutoff is located before a leak requires you to find it in the dark. Plumbing arrangements and winter requirements vary, so follow local utility guidance or consult a qualified plumber rather than wrapping every visible pipe and declaring victory.

Roofs, Gutters and Drainage

Clear leaves and debris from gutters, downspouts and drains so autumn rain and melting snow can move away from the house. Check that downspouts discharge where water will not collect near the foundation. From the ground, look for loose shingles, damaged flashing or branches touching the roof. Anyone uncomfortable or unsteady on a ladder should hire qualified help; gravity has never been impressed by thrift.

Find the Drafts

On a cool or windy day, inspect doors and windows for noticeable drafts. Replace worn weatherstripping, use removable caulk where suitable and close curtains after dark. Draft stoppers may help beneath doors, but they should never block a required exit or ventilation opening. Before buying a complicated energy-saving device, make certain the window is fully closed.

Blankets, Coats and Winter Clothing

Bring out blankets, coats, gloves, hats and cold-weather bedding before the first genuinely cold morning. Wash what needs washing, mend loose buttons and check whether children have outgrown last year’s clothing. Match the gloves while both patience and daylight remain. Donate usable surplus where it will be welcomed, but discard items that are unsafe or beyond practical repair. A Wardrobe Observation: A household may own seven gloves and still possess no pair.

The Pantry Before Winter

Take inventory before adding supplies. Move older food forward, check package dates and plan several meals around what is already present. Stock practical foods the household actually eats rather than an impressive collection destined to survive every emergency and several changes of address. A modest reserve of familiar staples is more useful than a shelf of provisions nobody can identify without instructions.

Preparing for a Power Outage

Gather flashlights, fresh batteries, charged power banks, a battery-powered radio and safe drinking water. Keep necessary medications and basic pet supplies in mind, and ask a healthcare professional or pharmacist in advance how temperature-sensitive medicine should be handled during an outage. Write important contact numbers on paper in case a silent telephone also becomes an unhelpful address book.

Food During an Outage

Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed as much as possible. According to Ready.gov, refrigerated food generally remains safe for about four hours without power if the door stays closed. A full freezer may hold a safe temperature for about 48 hours, and a half-full freezer for about 24 hours. Use appliance thermometers when available, and discard food when safety is uncertain.

Generator Safety

A portable generator must never operate inside a home, basement, shed or garage—even with doors and windows open. The CDC advises using it outdoors, more than twenty feet from windows, doors and vents, with the exhaust directed away from the home. Use a working battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide detector indoors. A generator produces electricity and exhaust; only the first belongs in the house.

Pet and Animal Preparations

Count animals when planning food, water, warmth, medication and evacuation needs. Check bedding, carriers, identification and any cold-weather arrangements for outdoor animals. Antifreeze is dangerous to pets, and even a small spill should be cleaned promptly and stored securely. An animal cannot read the emergency plan, but it will certainly notice if the household forgot to include dinner.

The October Household Ledger

List likely winter expenses before they arrive: heating service, weatherstripping, chimney care, plumbing protection, batteries, pet supplies and clothing replacements. Separate urgent safety work from cosmetic wishes, estimate each cost and schedule the most important tasks first. A household budget is not merely a record of spending; it is a method of preventing every expense from arriving disguised as a surprise.

Barn Backyard and Wildwood

October’s Animals

October does not arrive quietly in the animal kingdom. Birds gather for journeys, squirrels bury fortunes they may never recover, deer become restless, hens exchange respectable feathers for a most undignified wardrobe, and household pets wonder why humans have begun placing pumpkins on the furniture. Every creature reads the season differently, but all understand the same message: winter is on its way.

Travelers in the Sky

Autumn migration continues through October, although its timing varies by species and location. Many birds travel at night, guided by stars, familiar landscapes and instincts inherited from countless generations. Turning off unnecessary outdoor lights, especially during peak migration, can help prevent birds from becoming confused or colliding with windows and buildings. Bird feeders may be kept through autumn, provided they are cleaned regularly and placed where spilled seed will not attract unwanted guests. Native plants are even better hosts: their seeds, berries and insects offer travelers the sort of meals nature intended. A tidy garden may please the eye, but a few standing seed heads can please a hungry finch considerably more.

Monarchs on the Move

In much of North America, monarch butterflies may still be seen traveling south during early or mid-October. Eastern monarchs journey toward central Mexico, while many western monarchs travel toward overwintering sites along the California coast. Late-blooming native flowers provide nectar for these remarkable travelers, who cannot complete a journey of thousands of miles on admiration alone. Gardeners can help by avoiding pesticides and growing native milkweed and nectar plants suited to their region. Milkweed feeds monarch caterpillars, while flowering plants feed the adults. One is the nursery; the other is the roadside inn.

Busy Squirrels and Quiet Neighbors

Squirrels spend much of October collecting and hiding nuts. They possess an excellent instinct for saving and a somewhat less reliable filing system. Some buried food is recovered during winter, while forgotten nuts may sprout into new trees. Rabbits, chipmunks and other small animals also seek food and shelter as nights grow colder. Leave wild nests, burrows and sheltered brush undisturbed whenever they pose no danger. Before moving a woodpile or clearing a quiet corner, look carefully; what appears to be clutter to a person may already be somebody’s winter address.

Deer in Autumn

In many regions, deer become more active during their autumn breeding season, though the exact timing differs by climate and location. Drivers should be especially watchful around dawn and dusk. If one deer crosses the road, slow down and expect another, because deer are social creatures and apparently consider crossing separately an unnecessary inconvenience. Homeowners should admire deer from a distance rather than feed them. Regular feeding can attract crowds, spread disease, damage nearby plants and encourage animals to linger near roads. Wild creatures generally fare best when allowed to remain properly wild.

Cats, Dogs and October Mischief

Cooler weather may invite longer walks, but shorter days make pets harder for motorists to see. Reflective collars, leashes or small safety lights can improve visibility. Identification tags should be readable, microchip information should be current, and cats are safest indoors—particularly on busy evenings when doors repeatedly open and costumed strangers roam the neighborhood. Fleas and ticks do not consult the calendar before retiring for the year. In many climates they remain active well into autumn, so preventive care should continue according to a veterinarian’s advice. Senior animals may also feel chilly or stiff sooner than younger ones and may appreciate a warm bed placed away from drafts.

Halloween Without a Veterinary Adventure

Chocolate, raisins, alcohol and products containing xylitol—sometimes listed as birch sugar—can be dangerous to pets. Candy wrappers may cause choking or intestinal blockage even after the candy has disappeared. Keep treats in closed containers beyond the reach of paws, noses and clever accomplices. Candles, electrical cords, dangling decorations and artificial cobwebs may also tempt curious animals. Costumes should be used only if a pet is comfortable, can move and breathe freely, and remains supervised. A festive bandanna is quite sufficient for an animal who regards trousers as a personal insult. Provide a calm room for pets who dislike doorbells, noise or unfamiliar visitors. Keep them indoors, check doors carefully and never leave an anxious animal alone in a costume. If a pet eats something suspicious, contact a veterinarian or animal poison-control service immediately instead of waiting for symptoms to appear.

Around the Barn and Coop

Before cold weather settles in, inspect roofs, fences, gates, bedding and water systems. Animals need dry shelter and protection from harsh wind, but barns and coops still require ventilation; sealing every crack may trap moisture and harmful gases inside. Store feed where it will remain dry and protected from rodents, and discard anything moldy or spoiled. Watch each animal’s body condition rather than trusting a winter coat to tell the whole story. Older, young, thin or ill animals may need special attention as temperatures fall. Hooves, teeth, parasite control and veterinary care should be addressed before winter turns a small problem into a cold and inconvenient one. Chickens commonly molt in autumn, shedding old feathers and growing new ones. Egg production may decline while their bodies devote energy to this work. A molting hen can look as though she has lost an argument with a feather pillow, but good-quality feed, clean water, dry shelter and patience are generally more useful than sympathy.

Country Lore—or Likely?

The saying: A woolly bear caterpillar with a broad brown band foretells a mild winter; a narrow band promises severe weather. The finding: Charming, but unreliable. The caterpillar’s coloring is influenced by its age, growth and local conditions—not by privileged access to the winter forecast. Observe the woolly bear with affection, but consult a meteorologist before purchasing six cords of firewood.

Frida’s Animal Accounting

Frida recommends keeping a small autumn animal ledger. Record pet medications, veterinary appointments, identification numbers, feed purchases and winter supplies. Farmers may add body-condition notes, hoof care, egg production and bedding needs. Backyard naturalists can record the first migrating flock, the last monarch and any new visitor to the feeder. According to Frida, every household needs an emergency reserve for food, medicine and unexpected veterinary expenses. She also advises maintaining a separate catnip allowance, though the editorial staff has been unable to confirm that this is a recognized financial principle.

A Thought for the Month

October belongs not only to the orchard and the garden, but also to the creatures preparing for the colder days ahead. Watch the sky, the hedgerow, the barn and the quiet corners of home. Every feather, paw and hoof has its own way of reading the season.

The Hunters Moon and October Sky

A Lantern Over the Harvest Fields

October evenings invite us outdoors. The air grows cooler, darkness arrives earlier, and the sky seems to have polished its stars for company. One need not own a telescope or understand complicated charts to enjoy it. The heavens have always been generous to anyone willing to look up.

October’s Moon Calendar

October begins with the Moon growing smaller, reaches its dark New Moon near the middle of the month, and ends beneath the light of the Hunter’s Moon. The official phase times below are given in Universal Time, so the local calendar date may differ slightly in some time zones. Last Quarter: October 3 at 13:25 UTC New Moon: October 10 at 15:50 UTC First Quarter: October 18 at 16:12 UTC Full Hunter’s Moon: October 26 at 04:12 UTC The New Moon brings the darkest skies and is the best time to search for faint stars. The First Quarter is especially pleasant through binoculars because shadows along the boundary between lunar night and day reveal mountains and craters. The Full Moon is grander, but its strong light hides many of the quieter stars—a reminder that even the brightest guest can dominate the conversation.

Why It Is Called the Hunter’s Moon

The Hunter’s Moon is traditionally the first full moon following the Harvest Moon. After crops had been gathered and fields cleared, its autumn light was associated with hunting and preparing provisions for winter. The name arose from seasonal life rather than from a single ceremony, and traditions varied among places and peoples. In 2026, the Harvest Moon falls on September 26, making the October 26 full moon the Hunter’s Moon. It will appear nearly full on the nights before and after its official phase, so readers need not abandon all hope if clouds arrive precisely on schedule.

Saturn Takes the Evening Stage

Saturn reaches opposition in early October 2026, placing Earth roughly between Saturn and the Sun. Around this time, the planet rises near sunset, remains visible through much of the night and appears brighter than usual. Look for a steady, pale golden light; unlike most stars, planets generally twinkle very little. A modest telescope may reveal Saturn’s rings, though their appearance changes as the planet and Earth move around the Sun. Binoculars will not usually separate the rings, but they can help identify Saturn among surrounding stars. Saturn does not object to being admired with inexpensive equipment.

Venus Changes Shifts

Venus begins October low in the evening sky but moves closer to the Sun’s glare as the month advances. It passes between Earth and the Sun on October 24 and will soon return to the morning sky. Its visibility depends greatly on location, atmospheric conditions and an unobstructed horizon. Never sweep binoculars or a telescope near the Sun while searching for a planet. Wait until the Sun has completely set or follow guidance from an experienced astronomy group. Curiosity is a virtue; permanent eye damage is not.

Jupiter Before Breakfast

Jupiter shines in the morning sky during October, rising before the Sun and becoming easier to see as the month continues. It appears as a bright, steady point of light. Binoculars held steadily may reveal some of its four largest moons as tiny specks arranged beside it. The Moon passes near Jupiter during October, although the exact appearance and timing depend upon the observer’s location. A local planetarium chart or skywatching application can provide viewing times for a particular town. The sky follows one clock, but our horizons do not.

The Orionid Meteors

The Orionid meteor shower is expected to reach its annual peak around October 21. These swift meteors are produced by small particles left behind by Halley’s Comet. They appear to travel outward from the direction of Orion, but they may flash across any part of the sky. In 2026, a bright waxing Moon will interfere with the darker sky needed for ideal meteor watching. The best chance may come during the hours before dawn, once the Moon is lower or has set, depending on location. Find the darkest safe place available, allow the eyes at least twenty minutes to adjust, and look across a broad portion of the sky rather than staring directly at Orion. Meteor showers require patience. Some minutes produce nothing; the next may produce a brilliant streak. Bring a chair, a blanket and modest expectations.

October’s Starry Landmarks

From much of the Northern Hemisphere, the Great Square of Pegasus stands prominently in the autumn evening sky. Its four stars form a large, slightly crooked square that can guide the eye toward Andromeda. Under dark conditions, the Andromeda Galaxy may appear as a faint, misty patch—the light of an enormous galaxy traveling to us from far beyond our own Milky Way. Cassiopeia, shaped rather like a wide W, is another useful autumn guide. Later at night, Orion begins returning to the eastern sky, announcing that winter is waiting beyond the next page of the calendar. The Pleiades, or Seven Sisters, also rise during the evening and resemble a tiny dipper made from a handful of blue-white sparks.

A Beginner’s Evening Under the Stars

Choose a safe place with a broad view and as little artificial light as possible. Bring warm clothing, a reclining chair and a dim red flashlight, which preserves night vision better than bright white light. Begin with the unaided eye, then add binoculars if desired; many beautiful sights are too broad for a telescope. Give yourself time. The longer you remain away from bright screens and lamps, the more stars will appear.

Historical Mystery: The Nebra Sky Disc

More than three thousand years ago, someone in Bronze Age Europe fashioned a bronze disc decorated with gold shapes representing heavenly objects. Known today as the Nebra Sky Disc, it appears to show a crescent Moon, a full Moon or Sun, stars and perhaps the Pleiades. Golden arcs added to the disc may mark parts of the horizon connected with the yearly movement of the Sun. Scholars continue to study how the disc was used and what every symbol meant. It may have helped reconcile solar and lunar calendars, carried religious meaning or served several purposes during its long life. Its makers left no instruction booklet, which has preserved both the mystery and the employment of archaeologists. Evidence label: Well documented object; some interpretations remain uncertain.

Moon Lore—or Likely?

The saying: A ring around the Moon means rain or snow is coming soon. The finding: Sometimes likely. A lunar halo can form when moonlight passes through ice crystals in high, thin clouds. Such clouds may arrive ahead of a weather system, but the halo is not a guarantee of rain. It is evidence of ice crystals overhead—not a signed promise from the weather. The saying: Planting, harvesting or cutting hair by the Moon’s phase guarantees better results. The finding: Traditional lunar calendars are fascinating records of rural custom, but broad claims about guaranteed results are not supported by strong scientific evidence. The Moon unquestionably affects ocean tides; it has shown considerably less interest in scheduling haircuts.

Frida by Moonlight

Frida recommends placing a chair near a safe, closed window for October moon-watching. She maintains that no expensive observatory is required when one possesses a comfortable cushion, excellent night vision and servants willing to open the curtains. She also advises keeping a small sky ledger. Record the Moon’s phase, weather, visible planets, migrating birds and the first evening Orion appears. A year from now, these notes will turn an ordinary month into a personal almanac—and cost considerably less than most nighttime entertainment.

A Thought Beneath the Stars

The October sky joins past and present without asking permission. We may stand beneath it carrying phones, binoculars and modern star charts, yet the Moon, Saturn and the Pleiades are the same lights that guided farmers, sailors and storytellers long before us. Look upward, then return to tomorrow with a little more wonder than you carried into tonight.

Poetry Corner

John Keats and the Music of Autumn

John Keats was born in London on October 31, 1795. He trained in medicine before devoting himself to poetry, but his writing life was painfully brief. He died from tuberculosis in Rome in 1821 at only twenty-five years old. His years were few; his words have shown considerably greater endurance. Keats composed “To Autumn” while living in Winchester in September 1819. After walking through the countryside, he wrote of the stubble fields, temperate air and changing light he had observed. The poem does not treat autumn merely as the road to winter. It gives the season a complete life of its own—first abundant, then restful and finally musical.

To Autumn

By John Keats

I

Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness, Close bosom-friend of the maturing sun; Conspiring with him how to load and bless With fruit the vines that round the thatch-eves run; To bend with apples the moss’d cottage-trees, And fill all fruit with ripeness to the core; To swell the gourd, and plump the hazel shells With a sweet kernel; to set budding more, And still more, later flowers for the bees, Until they think warm days will never cease, For Summer has o’er-brimm’d their clammy cells.

II

Who hath not seen thee oft amid thy store? Sometimes whoever seeks abroad may find Thee sitting careless on a granary floor, Thy hair soft-lifted by the winnowing wind; Or on a half-reap’d furrow sound asleep, Drows’d with the fume of poppies, while thy hook Spares the next swath and all its twined flowers: And sometimes like a gleaner thou dost keep Steady thy laden head across a brook; Or by a cyder-press, with patient look, Thou watchest the last oozings hours by hours.

III

Where are the songs of Spring? Ay, where are they? Think not of them, thou hast thy music too,— While barred clouds bloom the soft-dying day, And touch the stubble-plains with rosy hue; Then in a wailful choir the small gnats mourn Among the river sallows, borne aloft Or sinking as the light wind lives or dies; And full-grown lambs loud bleat from hilly bourn; Hedge-crickets sing; and now with treble soft The red-breast whistles from a garden-croft; And gathering swallows twitter in the skies.

A Walk Through the Poem

The first stanza fills the table. Vines bend with fruit, apples weigh upon the trees, gourds swell, hazelnuts ripen and late flowers continue feeding the bees. Keats presents autumn not as a season of decline, but as nature’s well-stocked pantry. The second stanza allows the season to rest. Autumn sits upon the granary floor, sleeps beside the unfinished harvest and watches the last drops leave the cider press. There is work remaining, but no panic. The poem understands a truth that busy households occasionally forget: ripening cannot be improved by shouting at it. The final stanza asks where the songs of spring have gone, then answers that autumn possesses music of its own. Gnats, lambs, crickets, robins and swallows form the evening choir. None is especially grand alone, but together they make a season.

Words from the Old Orchard

Keats reminds us that beauty does not belong only to beginnings. A blossom is lovely, but so is the apple it becomes. Spring promises; autumn accounts for what the year has done with the promise. October asks us to notice what has ripened, what should be gathered and what may now be released. That lesson applies equally well to gardens, households and human hearts. There is a proper time to begin, a proper time to labor and a proper time to sit beside the cider press without demanding another apple.

An October Reading

Read the poem once indoors, then again near an open window or during a quiet walk. Listen for the small sounds Keats noticed: insects in the grass, birds gathering overhead, leaves moving in the wind and the distant business of animals. The poem becomes clearer when the season is permitted to read along. “To Autumn” is in the public domain.

Country Lore or Likely

Old Sayings Placed Under a Modern Magnifying Glass

Before weather satellites, radar and hourly forecasts, people watched the sky, plants and animals for signs of what might come next. Some observations contained practical wisdom. Others merely gave an innocent onion responsibility for the entire winter. The purpose of weather lore was not foolishness. It was an early attempt to recognize patterns. The mistake came when a useful observation hardened into an unbreakable rule—as human beings have always been fond of doing.

“Red Sky at Night, Sailors’ Delight”

The saying: A red sunset promises fair weather, while a red sunrise warns that poor weather is approaching. The finding: Sometimes likely. In regions where weather systems generally travel from west to east, a red evening sky may indicate clearer, drier air approaching from the west. A red morning sky may illuminate moisture-bearing clouds moving in from that direction. The saying is not dependable everywhere, but it has enough science behind it to retain its respectable seat by the window. “Count the Cricket’s Chirps”

“A Heavy Crop of Acorns Means a Hard Winter”

The saying: When oak trees produce abundant acorns, severe winter weather is coming. The finding: Lore. Oaks sometimes produce unusually large crops called mast years, but these are influenced by the trees’ biological cycles, pollination and growing conditions—not knowledge of future snowfall. A great acorn crop predicts that squirrels will be unusually busy. Beyond that, the oak declines to comment.

“Thick Onion Skins, a Cold Winter Comes In”

The saying: Thick or numerous onion skins foretell a severe winter; thin skins promise a mild one. The finding: Lore. An onion’s growth and outer layers reflect its variety, maturity, soil and weather during the growing season. They do not reveal conditions several months ahead. An onion may improve a stew, but it has not been accepted into the National Weather Service.

“Pine Cones Close Before Rain”

The saying: Pine cones close when wet weather approaches and open when dry weather returns. The finding: Likely—with limits. Pine-cone scales respond to moisture in the surrounding air. They tend to close in damp conditions, helping protect seeds, and open as the air becomes drier. A cone can therefore reveal present humidity, but it does not necessarily know whether rain will arrive tomorrow. It is more moisture gauge than prophet.

“A Clear October Night Brings Frost by Morning”

The saying: A cloudless, still autumn night is likely to end with frost. The finding: Often likely. Clouds act somewhat like a blanket, slowing the escape of heat from the ground. On clear, calm nights, the surface can cool rapidly, especially in low places where colder air collects. Frost may form even when the official air temperature remains slightly above freezing because thermometers and tender leaves do not always spend the night at the same height. Gardeners should heed this sign more seriously than the onion. Cover tender plants before bedtime, secure the covering and remove it when temperatures rise the next day.

“Cows Lie Down Before Rain”

The saying: A field full of reclining cattle means rain is coming. The finding: Unreliable. Cattle lie down for many excellent reasons, including rest, rumination and the simple possession of legs that would rather not stand all day. Weather may affect animal behavior, but a resting herd is not a trustworthy forecast by itself. The cows may know something—or they may merely be off duty.

“Spiders Spin Larger Webs Before Fine Weather”

The saying: Active spiders and large webs promise fair conditions. The finding: Possible observation, poor forecast. Spiders may change their activity in response to temperature, humidity, wind and insect availability. Calm weather can make web-building easier, but a newly constructed web does not guarantee several fair days ahead. It does guarantee that one spider has completed a considerable piece of work without requesting a building permit.

Keep a Weather-Lore Ledger

Choose three old sayings and test them during October. Record the date, the sign observed and the weather that follows during the next day or two. At the end of the month, count the successes, failures and occasions when the evidence was too uncertain to judge. This little experiment turns folklore into observation and may reveal why a saying survives. Even a poor predictor can occasionally be correct, particularly when given enough autumns and a forgiving audience.

A Word from the Fencepost

Old weather sayings deserve neither blind faith nor complete dismissal. They preserve generations of attention to land, sky and season. Some contain a kernel of truth, some contain only a good story, and a few contain enough of both to remain at the kitchen table. Observe carefully, test cheerfully and keep the reliable lessons. As for the rest, a little harmless folklore may warm an October evening even when it cannot predict a cold November morning.

Fridas Savings Corner

Small Savings, Warm Homes and Bigger Dreams

Frida believes money behaves rather like a cat: if nobody watches it, it may disappear into an unexpected corner. Her method is not to forbid every pleasure, but to give each dollar a sensible place to sit before it wanders away. October is an excellent month to review the household ledger. Heating costs, winter supplies and holiday plans are approaching, but there is still time to prepare without panic. A small amount saved now may prevent a much less charming expense later.

Plan wisely. Save happily. Dream a little bigger.

In Loving Memory of Frida

Frida was inspired by a much-loved Balinese cat whose memory continues to bring warmth, companionship and a little mischief to these pages.

The October Money Check-In

Set aside fifteen quiet minutes and look at the previous month’s spending. Do not begin by scolding yourself. Begin by observing. Which expenses were necessary, which were enjoyable and which have already been forgotten? A forgotten purchase is often an excellent clue about where next month’s savings may be hiding. Check upcoming bills, subscription renewals, seasonal expenses and annual payments. Write down the amount and due date for each. A bill placed upon a calendar is still a bill, but it is less likely to leap from behind the sofa.

Frida’s 31-Day Savings Challenge

Choose one amount and set it aside each day in October: Gentle challenge: $1 per day = $31 Steady challenge: $2 per day = $62 Big-dream challenge: $5 per day = $155 Use a separate savings account, an envelope or a clearly labeled jar. The amount matters less than the habit. Thirty-one deliberate deposits teach the hand to save before it teaches the purse to grow. Anyone with an irregular income may save on good days and pause on difficult ones. Missing a day is not failure; it is merely a blank square waiting for another opportunity. Frida has never abandoned a nap because she started seven minutes late.

Give the Savings a Name

Money is easier to protect when it has a purpose. Instead of labeling the fund simply Savings, call it Winter Heating, Holiday Meals, Pet Care, Emergency Cushion or January Peace. A named dollar is less likely to be reassigned to an attractive object discovered at eleven o’clock at night. If several needs are competing, choose one modest priority for October. Finish the month with one useful fund rather than six nearly empty envelopes and a sense that arithmetic has become personal.

Begin the Holiday Ledger Now

Write down every holiday expense you reasonably expect: gifts, food, travel, postage, decorations, charitable giving, school events and family gatherings. Then decide upon one total amount the household can afford without borrowing or neglecting regular bills. Divide that total among the categories before shopping begins. If one category grows, another must shrink. This rule lacks glamour, but it has rescued many a January from December’s enthusiasm. Keep a simple gift list with columns for the recipient, planned item, spending limit, amount paid and whether the gift has been wrapped or mailed. A good list prevents duplicate purchases, forgotten packages and the discovery that three people have somehow received mugs while Aunt Clara has received nothing.

Shop the Pantry First

Before making the grocery list, inspect the refrigerator, freezer and cupboards. Place foods that should be used soon in one visible Eat First area. Plan several meals around what is already present before purchasing more. A forgotten can of beans is not a bargain merely because it was bought on sale. Food saves money only when it is eaten. Frida therefore recommends taking inventory before shopping, although she considers unopened tuna exempt from all delay.

Make One Ingredient Do Several Jobs

Choose ingredients that can appear in more than one meal. Roasted vegetables may accompany supper, fill tomorrow’s soup and join eggs the following morning. Cooked chicken may become sandwiches, dumplings or a casserole. Apples may serve as snacks, sauce, breakfast or dessert. This is not the same as eating an identical meal for seven consecutive days. Economy should reduce waste, not extinguish morale.

Build a Modest Winter Pantry

Purchase winter staples gradually and only when the household will genuinely use them. Flour, oats, rice, beans, canned tomatoes, broth and shelf-stable milk may be useful, but an impressive row of unwanted food is merely expensive decoration. Mark packages with the purchase date and place newer items behind older ones. Keep a small inventory so that the pantry does not acquire nine jars of cinnamon while somehow possessing no salt.

Prepare the House Before Paying to Heat It

October is a good time to check doors and windows for drafts, replace worn weatherstripping and schedule necessary heating-system maintenance. Close curtains at night, open them to useful sunlight during the day and keep furniture from blocking heating vents or radiators. Set the thermostat to the lowest comfortable temperature appropriate for the household, health needs and type of heating system. Extreme settings are neither necessary nor wise. A sweater may be economical; seeing one’s breath in the sitting room suggests the experiment has gone too far.

The Repair-or-Replace Pause

When something breaks, pause before buying a replacement. Ask whether it can be cleaned, repaired, borrowed or purchased secondhand. Then compare the repair cost, expected remaining life, safety and energy use with the price of replacement. Cheap repairs are not always economical, and new purchases are not always wasteful. The purpose is to make the decision deliberately rather than allowing inconvenience to do the shopping.

Keep a Small Pet-Care Fund

Food, medication, vaccinations and unexpected veterinary care deserve a place in the household plan. Even a few dollars set aside regularly can soften the cost of an urgent visit. Record medications, appointments and identification information where they can be found quickly. Frida strongly approves this category. She has suggested that it be placed above mortgage, groceries and electricity, but the editorial staff has restored the original order.

The October Ledger

At the end of the month, record: Amount saved Food used before it spoiled Subscriptions canceled or changed Winter supplies purchased Holiday spending planned One repair made instead of one item replaced One small pleasure enjoyed without regret Progress need not be dramatic to be real. A household grows stronger through repeated ordinary decisions—the meal made from the pantry, the draft stopped before winter and the five dollars placed where tomorrow can find it.

Frida’s Final Word

Save something, however small. Waste less than last month. Prepare for one expense before it becomes an emergency. Then make tea, sit somewhere comfortable and admire the account. A fortune may begin with grand ambition, but a peaceful household is more often built from little sums that were given useful work.
