Are American Households Spending Their Working Lives Building Estates—or Servicing Other People's Assets?

Americans owe more than $5 trillion in consumer credit. What did all that borrowing buy, who benefits from the payments—and what will remain for the next generation?

Research & drafting: OpenAI's ChatGPT
Editing & publication: MoneyTrackTemplates
Research reviewed: September 29, 2026

Two Lenders

In 1946, It's a Wonderful Life gave America two visions of lending.

George Bailey lent money because he wanted families to own homes and build lives.

Mr. Potter understood lending differently.

For Potter, other people's financial dependence was useful.

The distinction was not simply between a nice banker and a nasty banker. It was between two ideas about money.

Does finance serve the household?

Or does the household eventually exist to serve finance?

Eighty years later, perhaps we should ask the question again.

Because Americans now owe more than $5 trillion in consumer credit.

So who is all that debt working for?

Five Trillion Dollars

Federal Reserve G.19 data put consumer credit outstanding at more than $5 trillion in 2026.

The figure includes revolving credit, such as credit-card balances, and nonrevolving credit, such as many automobile, student and personal loans. It does not include mortgages and other loans secured by real estate.

Broaden the lens to total household debt and the number becomes much larger.

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York reported approximately $18.8 trillion in total household debt in the second quarter of 2026. Mortgages accounted for by far the largest portion, followed by automobile loans, student loans, credit cards and other household obligations.

Before we become accustomed to those numbers, perhaps we should remain slightly shocked by them.

How Did We Get Here?

Americans borrow to buy homes.

We borrow to buy automobiles.

We borrow for college.

We borrow on credit cards.

We borrow for emergencies.

We finance furniture.

We finance appliances.

We finance phones.

We divide ordinary purchases into Buy Now, Pay Later installments.

Some borrowing acquires assets.

Some borrowing pays for opportunities.

Some borrowing gets a family through a genuine emergency.

And some borrowing purchases something that will be gone long before the final payment is.

The financial question isn't whether every loan is foolish.

It isn't.

The question is what happens when borrowing stops being an occasional financial tool and becomes the ordinary mechanism through which a household lives.

The Household That Is Already Spent

Imagine payday arrives Friday.

But before Friday arrives:

The mortgage has a claim.

The automobile lender has a claim.

The credit-card company has a claim.

The student-loan servicer has a claim.

The Buy Now, Pay Later companies have their claims.

Perhaps a personal lender has one too.

The paycheck has arrived.

But portions of it have already been promised to people the worker may never meet.

That is the part of debt we rarely discuss.

Debt doesn't merely consume money.

It makes claims on future income.

The more income already promised, the less tomorrow belongs to the household that earned it.

Who Got Richer?

This is where the MoneyTrackTemplates reader should become slightly impolite.

If Americans owe trillions of dollars, somebody owns trillions of dollars of financial claims.

Federal Reserve G.19 statistics don't merely report how much consumer credit exists. They also report major categories of institutions holding those claims, including depository institutions, credit unions, finance companies and the federal government.

To the household:

Debt.

On the other side of the ledger:

An asset or financial claim.

That does not make the holder villainous.

Banks, credit unions and other lenders provide capital and assume risks. Interest compensates lenders for providing money today in exchange for repayment later.

But we should understand the arrangement.

When you pay interest, the interest doesn't evaporate.

When you pay a fee, the fee doesn't vanish.

Money leaves one balance sheet and arrives on another.

So follow it.

The Potter Question

Who becomes financially stronger because I borrowed this money?

Sometimes the answer includes the borrower.

A mortgage may help create home equity.

An education loan may finance skills that increase earning capacity.

A reliable automobile may make employment possible.

In those situations, lender and borrower may both benefit.

Very George Bailey.

But consider another loan.

The borrower receives temporary purchasing power.

The purchase is quickly consumed.

Interest accumulates.

Fees are collected.

The borrower refinances.

Or borrows again.

Or carries a balance into another year.

Now ask:

Whose balance sheet became stronger?

That's the Potter question.

Not:

“Is this lender evil?”

That's an opinion.

Ask:

“Who accumulated the asset?”

That's accounting.

Are We Buying Assets—or Payments?

There is an enormous difference between borrowing money for something that may build household wealth and borrowing for something that disappears while the payments remain.

One may leave an asset behind.

The other may leave receipts.

That doesn't mean every depreciating purchase is irresponsible.

People need automobiles.

Appliances break.

Emergencies happen.

Children need braces.

Life has an irritating habit of sending invoices without first consulting the household budget.

But across millions of households, the distinction matters enormously.

Are we using debt to acquire things that strengthen the household balance sheet?

Or are we increasingly using debt simply to maintain consumption?

Because one path can leave something behind.

The other can leave another payment.

What Are We Leaving the Children?

For generations, the aspiration was fairly simple.

Work.

Acquire something.

Pay it off.

Save something.

Leave something behind.

Perhaps a house.

Perhaps land.

Perhaps investments.

Perhaps a business.

Perhaps merely enough savings that the children don't have to pay for the funeral.

But what happens when a lifetime becomes increasingly financed?

Children generally do not automatically become personally responsible for their parents' individual debts merely because they are their children. Ordinarily, debts are paid from the deceased person's estate.

If the estate cannot pay and nobody else is legally responsible for the debt, the debt generally goes unpaid. Exceptions can apply, including certain jointly held or co-signed obligations and circumstances governed by state law.

That sounds reassuring.

Until you remember what an estate is.

The estate is the money and property the parent hoped to leave behind.

They May Not Inherit the Debt. They May Inherit Less.

Suppose a parent dies owning:

A house.

A savings account.

Some investments.

Personal property.

And substantial unpaid debts.

The children ordinarily don't receive a neat little box marked:

MOM'S VISA BILL — YOUR PROBLEM NOW

But valid creditor claims may have to be paid from the estate before remaining property passes to heirs, depending on the debt and applicable law.

So debt can reach across generations differently.

Not necessarily by becoming the child's personal obligation.

But by reducing what remains.

The child didn't inherit the loan.

The loan helped determine the inheritance.

Now that enormous national debt figure looks rather different.

What Did Grandma Leave?

Imagine two households.

Over their working lives, both earned roughly similar amounts.

One reaches the end with:

A paid-off house.

Savings.

Investments.

Few remaining debts.

The other reaches the end with:

A heavily financed house.

Automobile debt.

Credit-card balances.

Personal loans.

Little savings.

Their children may technically inherit no personal liability for most individual debts.

But the two families are not passing forward the same economic position.

One generation accumulated assets.

The other spent decades servicing financial claims.

And that difference can continue long after the original borrower is gone.

Wealth Compounds Too

We hear a great deal about compound interest when discussing debt.

But ownership compounds too.

A house can become equity.

Investments can produce returns.

Savings can produce interest.

A business can become an asset.

Property can pass to another generation.

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances documents substantial differences in household wealth across American families.

Its most recent full survey covers 2022, so we should not pretend those measurements describe household wealth in 2026. But it provides an important framework for studying assets and liabilities together rather than examining debt alone.

That is the other side of the ledger.

Debt can compound.

Ownership can compound.

Which one is the household accumulating?

The Question We Should Measure

This is where our fictional comparison becomes useful without turning into a verdict.

We don't need MoneyTrackTemplates to announce whether modern America is Bedford Falls or Pottersville.

Instead, we investigate.

Are household assets growing faster than liabilities?

Which kinds of debt are increasing?

Who holds those debts?

What proportion of household income is committed to servicing them?

Where are delinquencies rising?

Where are incomes rising?

Which states are producing greater economic growth?

Which industries are creating that growth?

And:

Who owns what?

Those are measurable questions.

Which means our little robots have work to do.

Follow Both Sides of the Ledger

Perhaps this is where financial education has sometimes stopped too soon.

We teach people:

Make the payment.

Build the credit score.

Qualify for the loan.

Refinance the loan.

Those things can be useful.

But there's another question:

What are you building?

If thirty years of mortgage payments eventually produce an owned home, that's something.

If years of investing produce financial assets, that's something.

If education increases earning capacity, that's something.

But if decades of borrowing primarily produce additional borrowing, we should be willing to examine that too.

Not with shame.

With arithmetic.

Financial Independence May Have a Simpler Definition

Perhaps financial independence isn't having enough available credit to buy whatever we want.

Perhaps it is gradually reaching the point where fewer people have claims on tomorrow's paycheck.

The mortgage disappears.

The car belongs to you.

The credit-card balance is zero.

Savings belong to you.

Investments belong to you.

The house belongs to you.

Your future income increasingly belongs to you.

And someday, perhaps, something remains for somebody else.

That's a very different balance sheet.

For Young Researchers: Never Confuse Debt With Wealth

A household can have substantial debt and substantial assets.

Another can have little debt because it owns very little.

A wealthy household may carry a large mortgage.

A lower-income household may have no mortgage because it does not own a home.

Debt alone therefore cannot tell us whether a household is financially healthy.

Neither can income.

Neither can a credit score.

We need the balance sheet.

Assets minus liabilities = net worth.

Then we need context.

Who owns the assets?

Who owes the liabilities?

What produces the income?

Where does the interest go?

And how are those things changing over time?

Now we're investigating wealth rather than merely counting bills.

The MoneyTrack Rule

Whenever an impressive financial number appears on this website, ask:

What is being measured?

What period does it cover?

Whose money is it?

Who owes it?

Who owns the corresponding claim?

What did the transaction purchase?

What source produced the number?

When was it updated?

Our bots can handle the repetitive part.

Humans still have to ask the interesting questions.

That seems like a reasonable division of labor.

Bottom Line

More than $5 trillion in consumer credit represents more than a giant national number.

It represents millions of individual promises about future income and outcomes.

Some of those promises helped households acquire homes, education, transportation and other things of lasting value.

Others financed consumption.

All created claims on future money.

So don't merely ask:

How much do I owe?

Ask:

What did the debt buy?

Do I still own what it bought?

Is it becoming an asset?

Who receives my interest?

Who receives the fees?

Who owns my debt?

How much of my future income is already promised?

And someday:

What will remain after the creditors have been paid?

George Bailey wanted his customers to eventually own something.

Potter was quite comfortable owning the claims.

We don't need to decide which fictional town America resembles.

We have something considerably more useful.

A balance sheet.

Follow the debt.
Follow the assets.
Follow the ownership.
Follow the money.

Then check the receipts.

No political team required. Just the receipts. 🧾
