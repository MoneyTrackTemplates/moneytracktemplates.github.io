Who Holds America's Debt?

Forty trillion dollars, a very expensive dinner, and the extraordinary challenge of paying the bill.

MoneyTrackTemplates · Research-based editorial · October 2026

The Most Expensive Dinner in American History

Imagine that 340 million Americans attend an enormous dinner party.

Nobody remembers making the reservation. The menu has been revised approximately 250 times, and half the guests are arguing about appetizers they ordered decades ago.

Nevertheless, the dinner continues.

Eventually, a waiter arrives carrying the bill.

It is approximately $40 trillion.

An uncomfortable silence follows.

One guest suggests splitting the bill equally. Another demands to know who ordered the aircraft carriers. A third insists that someone at another table should pay because their entrée was more expensive.

Meanwhile, the waiter would like to discuss interest.

Welcome to America's national debt.

Unlike our fictional dinner party, the federal government has identifiable creditors, detailed financial records and an established system for borrowing money.

What it doesn't have is a magical method for making its accumulated obligations disappear.

So who owns the debt? What happens if borrowing continues? And how could the United States eventually balance its budget, generate a surplus and reduce what it owes?

Before anybody reaches for the dessert menu, let's examine the bill.

1. Who Actually Holds America's Debt?

The federal government borrows principally by issuing Treasury securities. Investors purchase these securities, and the government promises repayment according to their terms.

Treasury bills, notes and bonds are different forms of this borrowing.

Their owners include American households, retirement funds, banks, insurance companies, investment funds, foreign investors and the Federal Reserve.

In other words, the federal government's creditors are not a mysterious international organization meeting in an underground vault.

Some are Americans who own Treasury securities directly or indirectly through retirement accounts.

Others are financial institutions investing on behalf of their customers.

Foreign governments and private foreign investors also participate.

And the Federal Reserve holds Treasury securities as part of its monetary policy operations.

Imagine discovering that the government owes money to your retirement fund.

You are now simultaneously a taxpayer and, indirectly, a creditor.

Congratulations.

You've achieved the financial equivalent of lending money to your employer and then wondering why the company's expenses keep increasing.

Of course, taxpayers and bondholders aren't necessarily the same people. The benefits of interest income and the burden of taxation are distributed differently.

That distinction becomes important when examining who ultimately benefits from government borrowing.

2. The Government Owes Money to Itself?

The national debt has two major categories.

Debt held by the public consists primarily of Treasury securities held outside federal government accounts. It includes securities owned by domestic investors, foreign investors and the Federal Reserve.

Intragovernmental debt consists of Treasury securities held by federal government accounts, including certain trust funds.

Consider a fictional household.

Margaret deposits $500 into a savings account labeled EMERGENCIES.

Her husband, Harold, discovers the account and borrows $300 to repair the family car.

He leaves a handwritten note promising repayment with interest.

Margaret still has an asset: Harold's IOU.

Harold has a liability.

But the household hasn't suddenly become $300 wealthier.

Federal intragovernmental debt involves a more complicated legal and accounting structure, but the example illustrates why debt owed between government accounts must be distinguished from debt owed to outside investors.

There's another important detail.

The trust funds holding those securities have legal claims on the Treasury. Their assets are not imaginary simply because another part of the government owes the money.

Harold, please take note.

3. Foreign Creditors: Who Owns the Dining Room?

Foreign ownership of Treasury securities attracts considerable attention.

Japan, the United Kingdom and mainland China are among the major locations recorded in Treasury data.

But a Treasury security recorded in London is not necessarily owned by the British government. International financial institutions may hold securities on behalf of investors located elsewhere.

Furthermore, purchasing Treasury securities does not confer ownership of American territory, federal buildings or national policy.

If that were how government bonds worked, somebody would have acquired the Lincoln Memorial years ago.

Foreign investors are creditors, not shareholders in the United States.

Nevertheless, foreign borrowing has economic implications. Interest payments to foreign investors transfer income abroad, and changes in international demand can influence Treasury financing conditions.

The important question isn't simply which country holds the largest portfolio.

It's how much the government borrows, what that borrowing costs and how dependent its financing becomes on continued investor demand.

4. The Interest Bill Nobody Ordered

Now we reach the part of the dinner where everybody suddenly remembers an urgent appointment.

Interest.

Borrowing allows the government to finance expenditures without collecting an equivalent amount of revenue immediately.

But investors generally expect compensation for lending their money.

According to the Congressional Budget Office's February 2026 outlook, federal net interest expenses were projected to reach approximately $1 trillion in fiscal year 2026 and $2.1 trillion annually by 2036. [1]

Those are projections, not guaranteed outcomes. They depend on economic conditions, interest rates and the laws incorporated into the forecast.

Still, they illustrate the scale of the issue.

Imagine a household with a $100,000 annual income.

It has accumulated substantial debt, and its annual interest bill is growing.

Each year, more of its income goes toward servicing existing obligations.

The family can still purchase groceries, repair the roof and invest in its children's education.

But every additional dollar spent on interest is a dollar unavailable for another purpose unless the household earns more, cuts something else or borrows again.

The federal budget faces similar trade-offs, although a sovereign government is not financially identical to a household.

Governments can collect taxes, refinance debt and influence economic conditions through public policy. The United States also issues debt in its own currency.

Those differences matter.

Nevertheless, the underlying budget constraint remains: interest payments require financial resources.

And unlike Harold's promise to repay Margaret after his next paycheck, Treasury securities come with legally specified obligations.

The waiter is not interested in excuses.

5. What Happens if the Debt Keeps Growing?

There is an important distinction between borrowing money and borrowing more money faster than the economy can grow.

Government debt is not automatically harmful.

Borrowing can help finance infrastructure, respond to emergencies, support economic activity during recessions and distribute the cost of long-lived public investments across time.

The problem becomes more complicated when debt and interest expenses continue growing relative to the government's economic resources.

The Congressional Budget Office projected that debt held by the public would rise from approximately 101% of gross domestic product (GDP) in 2026 to 120% in 2036 under its February 2026 baseline. [1]

GDP measures the value of goods and services produced by an economy.

Comparing debt with GDP provides more information than examining the debt figure alone.

Consider two fictional businesses.

One owes $1 million and earns $10 million annually.

The other owes $1 million and earns $100,000.

Their debts are identical.

Their financial circumstances are decidedly not.

The federal government's finances are considerably more complicated, but the debt-to-GDP ratio provides a useful measure of debt relative to economic activity.

What consequences might arise if that ratio continues increasing?

First, interest expenses may consume a larger share of federal resources. This creates difficult decisions about taxation, spending and further borrowing.

Second, persistent government borrowing can place upward pressure on interest rates and reduce private investment under some economic conditions. The size of these effects depends on economic circumstances and monetary policy.

Third, the government may have less fiscal flexibility during future emergencies. Responding to a recession, natural disaster or national security crisis becomes more complicated when existing obligations already require substantial resources.

Fourth, future taxpayers and policymakers inherit obligations created by earlier decisions. They also inherit whatever public investments, institutions and economic benefits those decisions produced.

And finally, a sustained loss of investor confidence could increase borrowing costs or create financial instability.

These outcomes are risks, not a timetable for an inevitable catastrophe.

America is not scheduled to go bankrupt next Thursday.

But neither does postponing difficult fiscal decisions automatically make them less expensive.

6. The Deficit: America's Annual Restaurant Tab

Now we must distinguish between two numbers frequently confused in public discussions.

The national debt is the accumulated stock of outstanding federal obligations.

The annual budget deficit is the amount by which federal spending exceeds federal revenue during a particular fiscal year.

Imagine that our enormous American dinner party already owes the restaurant $40 trillion.

During the next year, it orders another $7 trillion worth of food and collects only $5 trillion from its guests.

The new annual deficit is $2 trillion.

The accumulated debt increases because the restaurant must finance the unpaid portion of the bill.

The Congressional Budget Office's February 2026 baseline projected a federal deficit of approximately $1.9 trillion in fiscal year 2026, increasing to $3.1 trillion in 2036 under the laws and assumptions used in its forecast. [1]

Now consider an uncomfortable question.

If the government cannot cover its current annual expenditures, how will it begin reducing accumulated debt?

The first step is understanding the difference between slowing the growth of debt and actually paying it down.

They are not the same achievement.

Neither is accomplished merely by announcing that somebody has discovered wasteful spending.

Although eliminating genuine waste can certainly improve the budget, every proposed saving must be measured against the actual size of the deficit.

Canceling a $10 million program might be financially justified.

But it does not eliminate a trillion-dollar shortfall.

Our dinner guests have saved money by declining the complimentary breadsticks.

Unfortunately, they still owe the restaurant approximately $40 trillion.

7. How Do You Actually Create a Surplus?

Here is where the arithmetic becomes interesting.

A federal budget surplus occurs when government revenues exceed government outlays during a fiscal year.

Those outlays include net interest expenses.

Let's create a fictional federal budget, using much smaller numbers so nobody requires medical assistance.

In this hypothetical example, the government collects $200 billion more than it spends.

That is a genuine overall budget surplus.

It could use the excess resources to reduce outstanding debt, subject to the government's financing arrangements and other transactions.

But there's another concept: the primary surplus.

A primary surplus occurs when government revenue exceeds spending excluding net interest.

For example, suppose our fictional government collects $6 trillion, spends $5.5 trillion on programs and pays $800 billion in net interest.

It has a $500 billion primary surplus.

Excellent news?

Partially.

Once interest is included, it still has a $300 billion overall deficit.

Our dinner party has finally stopped ordering more food than it can afford.

Unfortunately, it still cannot cover the financing charges from previous meals.

This distinction explains why balancing the federal budget can require more than reducing ordinary program expenditures.

The government must account for interest on its accumulated obligations.

8. Four Ways to Change the Arithmetic

There is no single mechanical formula for eliminating the federal deficit.

Different combinations of economic growth, revenue changes, spending changes and borrowing costs can alter the fiscal outlook.

Each approach has consequences and trade-offs.

Economic growth. A larger economy can generate additional tax revenue even without increasing tax rates.

For example, a business that expands production, hires employees and generates additional profits may contribute more tax revenue.

However, growth does not automatically produce a surplus.

If government expenditures and interest costs increase faster than revenue, the deficit can persist despite a growing economy.

Our fictional dinner party has received a substantial pay raise.

Naturally, someone immediately proposes ordering lobster.

Changes to government revenue. Lawmakers can modify tax rates, tax bases, exemptions, enforcement and other revenue policies.

Different measures have different effects on households, businesses, investment incentives and economic activity.

Raising a particular tax rate does not necessarily produce a proportional increase in revenue because behavior and economic conditions can change.

Similarly, reducing a tax rate does not automatically generate enough additional economic activity to replace the revenue lost.

The actual effects require evidence, estimates and subsequent measurement.

Changes to government spending. Lawmakers can change appropriations, program eligibility, benefits, administrative expenses and other spending policies.

Some changes may produce immediate savings. Others take years to affect the budget.

A reduction in administrative waste is different from a reduction in retirement benefits, military procurement or medical assistance.

The fiscal savings and consequences must be examined separately.

Our dinner guests have agreed to eliminate unnecessary expenditures.

Unfortunately, nobody agrees on which expenses are unnecessary.

Changes to borrowing costs. Lower interest rates can eventually reduce financing costs as existing securities mature and the Treasury refinances them.

However, the federal government cannot simply order investors to accept lower yields.

Interest rates depend on economic conditions, inflation expectations, monetary policy, investor demand and other factors.

And lower borrowing costs alone cannot guarantee a surplus if spending continues exceeding revenue.

In practice, different fiscal strategies combine these elements.

The question isn't merely how much money a proposal saves or raises.

It's when those effects occur, how reliable the estimates are, who bears the consequences and whether the changes persist.

9. Must America Pay Off Every Dollar?

Here is a distinction that deserves more attention.

A government can improve its debt position without eliminating every outstanding Treasury security.

Suppose a fictional country has a $10 trillion economy and $10 trillion in publicly held debt.

Its debt-to-GDP ratio is 100%.

Over time, its economy grows to $15 trillion while its debt remains at $10 trillion.

Its debt-to-GDP ratio falls to approximately 67%.

The country hasn't eliminated its debt.

But its debt is now smaller relative to the size of its economy.

Alternatively, a government might continue borrowing while keeping debt growth slower than economic growth. Under suitable conditions, its debt-to-GDP ratio can still decline.

Of course, this does not mean interest costs disappear or that additional borrowing is automatically sustainable.

But it explains why economists frequently focus on stabilizing and reducing the debt-to-GDP ratio rather than insisting that all government debt must reach zero.

Treasury securities also serve important functions in financial markets. They provide investment instruments for households and institutions and are widely used as collateral and liquidity-management tools.

A world without any U.S. Treasury securities would therefore be quite different from today's financial system.

Eliminating the national debt and maintaining sustainable public finances are not identical objectives.

The waiter might be satisfied with a customer who consistently pays the bill, even if that customer continues using the restaurant.

10. Has America Ever Managed a Surplus?

Yes.

The United States recorded federal budget surpluses in fiscal years 1998, 1999, 2000 and 2001. [4]

Those surpluses reflected a combination of economic conditions, revenue growth and fiscal policy decisions.

They demonstrate that an annual federal surplus is possible.

They do not establish that the same conditions or policies could simply be reproduced today with identical results.

The economy, demographics, federal commitments, tax laws, interest costs and international circumstances have changed.

A historical example is evidence of possibility, not a ready-made instruction manual.

Imagine Harold announcing that he successfully balanced the family budget in 1999.

Margaret congratulates him.

Then she reminds him that their mortgage, medical expenses, income and family circumstances are somewhat different in 2026.

Harold quietly puts away his calculator.

11. What Would It Take to Reduce the Debt?

Let's return to our fictional dinner party.

The guests have finally agreed to examine the actual bill.

They discover that balancing next year's budget would stop them from adding another annual deficit, although other financing transactions can still affect the recorded debt.

Producing sustained overall surpluses could create resources to reduce outstanding obligations.

Economic growth could also reduce debt relative to GDP, even without eliminating the entire nominal balance.

The difficulty is that the necessary fiscal adjustments become larger when existing deficits and interest expenses continue accumulating.

In its February 2026 outlook, the Congressional Budget Office projected persistent deficits through 2036 under its baseline assumptions. [1]

That projection does not mean a surplus is mathematically impossible.

It means that, under the policies and assumptions incorporated into that forecast, a surplus does not emerge on its own.

Changing that trajectory would require different economic outcomes, fiscal policies or some combination of both.

There are also legitimate disagreements over the timing of fiscal adjustments.

Reducing deficits too aggressively during an economic downturn can weaken demand and employment.

Delaying adjustments indefinitely can increase interest costs and make later decisions more difficult.

The appropriate balance depends on economic conditions, policy objectives and the distribution of costs and benefits.

There is no financial law requiring every budget to be balanced every single year.

But persistent deficits have consequences, and those consequences deserve to be measured rather than dismissed.

12. The Waiter Returns

Our American dinner party has reached its final course.

The guests now understand that some of the restaurant's creditors are sitting at their own table.

They know the difference between this year's unpaid bill and the accumulated balance from previous dinners.

They have also discovered that reducing the annual deficit, producing a surplus and lowering the debt-to-GDP ratio are different objectives.

One guest proposes collecting more revenue.

Another proposes reducing expenditures.

A third argues that a larger economy would make the existing debt more manageable.

A fourth insists that interest costs deserve immediate attention.

Each proposal involves measurable effects and economic trade-offs.

For the first time all evening, somebody suggests examining the numbers before ordering anything else.

Progress.

Unfortunately, the waiter has returned with another statement.

Interest has accrued.

The lesson isn't that all government borrowing is inherently harmful or that America must eliminate every Treasury security.

It's that borrowing creates obligations, and those obligations influence future choices.

A sustainable fiscal position requires the government to consider how much it borrows, what that borrowing finances, how much it costs and whether future economic resources can support it.

And perhaps the most important question of all:

If tomorrow's taxpayers inherit today's bill, what will they receive in return?
