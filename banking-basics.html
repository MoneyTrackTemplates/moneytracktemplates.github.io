Banking Basics: How the U.S. Banking System Actually Works

A plain-English guide to banks, credit unions, financial apps, foreign-owned banks, deposit insurance, regulation, ownership, and the public records behind your money.

Research & drafting: OpenAI's ChatGPT
Editing & publication: MoneyTrackTemplates
Research reviewed: September 26, 2026

Most of us use the banking system without thinking very much about what happens behind the screen.

A paycheck appears in a checking account. A debit card buys groceries. A mortgage payment disappears automatically. Someone sends money through an app. Another person deposits a check by taking a photograph of it.

It all looks rather simple.

It isn't.

Behind those everyday transactions is an enormous network of banks, credit unions, financial technology companies, corporate holding companies, foreign banking organizations, government regulators, payment networks, deposit-insurance systems, laws, and financial records.

And today there are two complications that aren't always obvious from a company's name or its mobile app:

The company providing something that looks like banking may not actually be a bank.

And a bank operating in the United States may ultimately be controlled by a banking organization headquartered in another country.

Neither fact automatically makes an institution unsafe.

But both are excellent reasons to learn exactly who has your money.

Understanding modern banking therefore requires more than knowing how to balance a checking account.

We need to know who holds our deposits, who owns or controls the institution, where the parent organization is located, which laws and regulators apply, what insurance protects the deposit, and where we can verify all of this ourselves.

What Does a Bank Actually Do?

At its simplest, a bank accepts deposits and provides financial services such as loans, payments, and transfers.

But there is an interesting accounting detail hiding inside something as ordinary as a checking account.

Suppose you deposit $1,000.

You probably think of that $1,000 as an asset—and from your perspective, it is.

From the bank's perspective, however, your deposit is generally a liability. The bank owes that money to you.

Loans work in the opposite direction. If the bank lends a customer $20,000 for a car, the loan generally becomes an asset on the bank's balance sheet because the borrower owes money to the institution.

Your deposit is your asset, but it is generally the bank's liability.

Banks also hold securities and other assets, process payments, make loans, collect interest and fees, and participate in the broader financial system.

This is why a bank is much more than a building containing everybody's money in a very large vault.

Banks and Credit Unions Are Not the Same Thing

Banks and credit unions can provide many similar services, but their organizational structures are different.

Credit unions are cooperative financial institutions. The National Credit Union Administration (NCUA) regulates federal credit unions and administers the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund (NCUSIF).

As we develop this library, we will distinguish among commercial banks, savings institutions, credit unions, financial technology companies, foreign banking organizations, U.S. subsidiaries of foreign banking organizations, and U.S. branches of foreign banks.

They may all participate in the American financial system.

They are not necessarily the same kind of institution.

Who Regulates a Bank?

Here comes our first bowl of alphabet soup.

There isn't one government agency called "the bank regulator."

Major federal participants include the:

  • Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)
  • Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, commonly called the Federal Reserve Board
  • Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)
  • National Credit Union Administration (NCUA)
  • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)

State regulators also play important roles.

Foreign banking organizations operating in the United States introduce another layer of supervision and corporate structure.

Instead of asking only, "Who regulates banks?", ask:

"What exact institution am I dealing with, how is it chartered, who controls it, and which regulator has jurisdiction over it?"

What Does Federal Deposit Insurance Actually Protect?

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) protects eligible deposits at FDIC-insured institutions if the insured institution fails.

The standard insurance amount is currently $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category.

Every part of that sentence matters.

It does not simply mean that every account automatically receives a separate $250,000 of protection.

Deposit insurance is also different from investment protection. Stocks, bonds, mutual funds, cryptocurrency, and other investments do not become FDIC-insured deposits merely because they were purchased through a financial company.

Consumer research rule:
Verify the exact institution holding the deposit and the insurance applicable to the particular account. Do not rely solely on a familiar company name or logo.

🌎 The Bank Down the Street May Have a Foreign Parent

This is a part of American banking that many consumers may never have had reason to investigate.

A financial institution can operate in the United States while belonging to a banking organization headquartered elsewhere.

The Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council (FFIEC) defines a Foreign Banking Organization (FBO) as a foreign bank meeting specified forms of U.S. presence or control, including operating certain U.S. offices or controlling certain U.S. banking organizations.

Foreign banking organizations can participate in the United States through different structures.

  • A U.S. bank subsidiary
  • A branch of the foreign bank
  • An agency
  • Other qualifying banking or holding-company structures
A bank operating in America is not necessarily ultimately owned by an American banking organization.

That fact alone doesn't tell us whether the institution is safe or unsafe. It tells us that ownership is something worth identifying.

A Subsidiary Is Not the Same as a Branch

Suppose an international banking organization owns a separately incorporated bank in the United States.

The consumer may be dealing with that U.S. banking subsidiary.

Now imagine instead that a foreign bank operates a U.S. branch.

A branch is part of the foreign bank rather than a separately incorporated U.S. bank subsidiary.

An agency is another category and generally does not accept retail deposits in the manner of an ordinary deposit-taking branch.

These distinctions may sound like paperwork.

They aren't.

They help determine what legal institution you're actually doing business with and what regulatory and insurance framework applies.

⚠️ A U.S. Office Does Not Automatically Mean an FDIC-Insured Deposit

The Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council recognizes several categories of U.S. branches and agencies of foreign banking organizations, including:

  • Insured federal branches
  • Insured state branches
  • Uninsured federal branches
  • Uninsured state branches
  • Uninsured federal agencies
  • Uninsured state agencies
Seeing a foreign bank's office in an American city does not by itself establish that a particular account or financial product is FDIC-insured.

Verify the exact institution and the exact product.

🔎 Follow the Bank Across the Border

When MoneyTrackTemplates researches an institution with international ownership, we'll ask:

  • What is the exact legal name of the U.S. institution?
  • Is it a U.S.-chartered bank, foreign-bank branch, agency, or another type of entity?
  • Is this particular institution federally insured?
  • Is the customer's particular product an insured deposit?
  • Who is the immediate parent?
  • In what country is that parent organized?
  • Who is the ultimate or top-tier parent?
  • In what country is the top-tier organization located?
  • Which U.S. regulator supervises the American operation?
  • What foreign regulator supervises the parent organization?
  • What official enforcement or regulatory record exists?
  • Has the ownership structure changed?
  • What happens to eligible deposits if the insured institution fails?
  • What risks might arise if the foreign parent experiences financial distress?
  • What do the original records say?

🗺️ We Can Actually See the International Ownership Trail

This isn't theoretical.

The Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System publishes structure and share data for U.S. banking offices of foreign banking organizations.

The March 31, 2026 data identify U.S. offices and can show information such as the U.S. office location, foreign parent, country of the foreign parent, top-tier parent where applicable, and assets.

The public can trace an American banking office through portions of its international corporate structure using government records.

That is exactly the distinction we want readers to understand.

🌎 Foreign Ownership: What Are the Possible Benefits?

Foreign ownership is not automatically a disadvantage.

International banking organizations may provide access to global financial networks, international payments, trade finance, foreign currencies, cross-border services, and expertise serving customers and businesses operating in multiple countries.

Large international organizations may also operate across multiple markets rather than depending upon a single local market.

A U.S. subsidiary or qualifying insured branch can also operate within substantial American regulatory and supervisory frameworks.

We will not teach:

Foreign = dangerous.

We will teach:

Foreign = investigate the structure just as carefully as you would investigate domestic ownership.

⚠️ What Additional Questions Should Consumers Consider?

International ownership can introduce additional layers of complexity.

The financial health of a foreign parent can matter to the broader organization. Political, economic, currency, legal, or regulatory developments in another country can also affect an international banking group.

Corporate structures can make it harder for an ordinary consumer to determine which legal entity is responsible for a particular product.

Different entities within the same international banking group can also have different regulatory treatment and deposit-insurance status.

None of those facts establishes that a particular foreign-owned bank is unsafe.

They establish why institution-level research matters more than the nationality printed beside a parent's name.

🛡️ Our Consumer Rule: Insurance Before Nationality

If you're deciding where to place ordinary savings, the first question shouldn't simply be:

"Is this an American company?"

Nor should it simply be:

"Is this a foreign company?"

"What legal institution will hold my deposit, is that institution federally insured, what are the applicable coverage limits, and can I independently verify it?"

Then investigate ownership.

Then investigate financial condition and regulatory history.

Then examine fees, services, accessibility, interest rates, and other features.

Nationality alone doesn't answer those questions.

📱 When Your "Bank" Isn't Actually a Bank

Now we reach the other major complication in modern banking.

An app can let you receive money, send money, obtain a card, pay bills, or hold a balance.

It certainly feels like banking.

But a financial app isn't necessarily a bank.

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation warns that consumers increasingly encounter nonbank companies—often financial technology companies—that may have relationships with insured banks.

A nonbank company itself is not FDIC-insured. Funds may potentially become eligible for pass-through deposit insurance after being placed at an FDIC-insured bank and after applicable requirements are satisfied.

Important: Federal deposit insurance protects against failure of an FDIC-insured bank. It does not insure consumers against the bankruptcy or insolvency of a nonbank financial technology company itself.

What Does "Fintech" Mean?

Fintech is short for financial technology.

Financial technology companies use software and digital systems to provide or facilitate financial services.

Some are banks.

Some aren't.

Some partner with banks.

Some place customer funds at one or more banks.

Some provide payment services rather than traditional deposit accounts.

And some companies offer several different financial products, each of which may operate under a different legal and regulatory arrangement.

That's why MoneyTrackTemplates will avoid casually calling every financial app a "bank."

We'll determine what the product actually is.

🔎 Follow Your Money

For financial apps and digital financial products, we'll ask:

  • What exactly is the product?
  • What legal company operates it?
  • Is that company actually a bank?
  • Where are customer funds actually held?
  • What partner bank or banks are involved?
  • Is that bank domestically or foreign controlled?
  • What federal deposit insurance applies?
  • Who owns the financial technology company?
  • Who owns the partner bank?
  • Which regulators have jurisdiction?
  • What happens if the bank fails?
  • What happens if the financial technology company fails?
  • What happens if a foreign parent encounters distress?
  • Can the reader verify all of this independently?
When I see $2,000 on this screen, who legally has my $2,000, where is it being held, and what protects it if something fails?

Benefits and Risks of Financial Apps

Financial technology isn't inherently good or bad.

Possible benefits

  • Convenient mobile access
  • Easy person-to-person payments
  • Fast access to account information
  • Integration with phones and digital wallets
  • Reduced dependence on physical branches
  • Specialized financial tools and services

Questions and possible risks to investigate

  • Whether federal deposit insurance applies
  • Whether insurance depends upon pass-through requirements
  • Who maintains the records showing ownership of customer funds
  • Technology outages or account-access problems
  • Fraud and scam exposure
  • Privacy and data practices
  • Fees and transaction limits
  • Dispute procedures
  • What happens if the nonbank company encounters financial trouble
  • What happens if a partner bank fails
The question isn't "traditional bank good, app bad."

The question is:

What are you actually using, and what protections and risks come with it?

🔎 Who Owns the Bank?

The name printed on a bank branch doesn't necessarily tell you who ultimately controls the institution.

A bank can belong to a bank holding company.

That company can itself sit beneath another company.

The Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council (FFIEC) operates the National Information Center (NIC), which contains financial data and institution characteristics collected by the Federal Reserve System.

Institution profiles can include an Organization Hierarchy showing ownership relationships and an institutional history showing structural changes over time.

That lets the public investigate questions such as:

  • Who is the immediate parent?
  • Is there a bank holding company?
  • What organization sits at the top of the reported ownership chain?
  • Has ownership changed?
  • Was the institution acquired?
  • Did it merge?
  • Did it previously operate under another name?
  • Is the parent domestic or foreign?

A Connection Is Not Automatically a Conflict

This is one of our most important research rules.

Foreign ownership isn't evidence of wrongdoing.

Government ownership isn't evidence of wrongdoing.

Private ownership isn't evidence of wrongdoing.

Institutional investment isn't evidence of wrongdoing.

A shared director isn't automatically evidence of wrongdoing.

A political connection isn't automatically evidence of wrongdoing.

These are facts or relationships that may warrant additional research.

When a regulator, court, inspector general, ethics authority, or another authoritative public body has formally found misconduct, improper influence, or a conflict, we will identify that finding and link readers to the underlying record.

Document the connection. Don't invent the conclusion.

What Happens When a Bank Fails?

Banks can fail.

That's not ancient history.

When an FDIC-insured bank fails, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) may be appointed receiver and handle the institution's resolution.

Another financial institution may acquire assets or assume deposits, depending upon the resolution.

The FDIC maintains historical records of failed banks, allowing researchers to examine failures rather than relying on memories or headlines.

Eventually, our research will be able to follow:

Institution → Owner → Regulator → Failure → Receiver → Acquiring Institution → Surviving Organization

📜 A Lesson From Financial Crises

The reason we're teaching readers to investigate insurance, ownership, and institutional structure is that financial systems occasionally experience periods when these distinctions suddenly become extremely important.

During the Great Depression, the United States experienced severe banking instability and large numbers of bank suspensions.

Congress enacted the Banking Act of 1933, which established the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC).

Decades later, the financial crisis of 2007–2008 again demonstrated how problems involving mortgages, securities, financial institutions, liquidity, and interconnected markets could spread far beyond the institution where a particular risk originated.

We will examine both periods separately and in much greater detail elsewhere in this library.

The lesson isn't:

"Be afraid of banks."

The lesson is:

Know where your money is before a crisis gives you a reason to find out.

📊 Public Administration Research Note

Definitions Matter

International banking gives researchers several important methodological warnings.

Physical location is not the same as country of ownership.

Country of immediate parent is not necessarily country of ultimate parent.

Foreign ownership is not the same as foreign charter.

Foreign charter is not the same as uninsured status.

A U.S. subsidiary of a foreign banking organization is not the same legal structure as a U.S. branch of the foreign bank.

Federal deposit insurance should be verified for the actual institution and product rather than inferred from a familiar brand.
