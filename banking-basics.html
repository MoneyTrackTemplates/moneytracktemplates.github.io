How the U.S. Banking System Actually Works

Banks, credit unions, regulators, deposit insurance and banking law explained for the person who was never handed an instruction manual.

Research & drafting: OpenAI's ChatGPT
Editing & publication: MoneyTrackTemplates
Research reviewed: September 24, 2026

Most of us learn how to use a bank account long before anyone explains what a bank actually is. We deposit a paycheck, use a debit card, pay bills and perhaps borrow money — but what is happening behind the scenes?

This guide starts at the beginning. It also shows you something many financial explanations leave out: where important banking rules came from and where you can examine the original government documents yourself.

Plain English: A bank is a financial institution that can accept deposits and provide financial services such as loans and payment services. Banks do not simply place everyone's deposited money into separate boxes in a vault. Deposits become part of the institution's funding, while customers retain claims on their account balances under the terms of their accounts and applicable law.

1. What Happens When You Deposit Money?

Suppose you deposit $100 into a checking account. Your bank records that it owes you $100 according to the terms of your account. From the bank's perspective, your deposit is a liability because the bank has an obligation to you.

Banks also hold assets. These can include loans, securities, reserves and other financial assets. Banks use deposits and other funding sources to support lending and other activities.

That is why modern banking is more complicated than a giant vault filled with individually labeled piles of cash.

Why this matters: Understanding the difference between a bank's assets and liabilities makes subjects such as bank runs, liquidity, capital and bank failures much easier to understand later.

2. Banks and Credit Unions Are Not Exactly the Same

Banks and credit unions can offer many similar consumer services, such as checking accounts, savings accounts and loans. But they are organized differently and may operate under different charters and regulatory structures.

One especially important distinction concerns federal deposit or share insurance. Eligible deposits at FDIC-insured banks are protected through the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. Federally insured credit unions use the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund, administered by the National Credit Union Administration.

🛡️ Deposit Insurance: A Beginner's Rule

Never assume that every financial product sold by a bank, credit union, brokerage, app or financial company is federally insured merely because the company handles money.

Insurance depends on the institution, account ownership category and type of financial product.

When in doubt, verify the institution and coverage with the appropriate federal agency.

3. Who Regulates a Bank?

There is no single answer for every bank in the United States. Regulation depends partly on how an institution is chartered and whether it is a member of the Federal Reserve System.

At the federal level, bank supervision is divided principally among the Federal Reserve, Office of the Comptroller of the Currency and Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. State banking authorities also supervise certain state-chartered institutions.

🏛️ OCC

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency charters, regulates and supervises national banks and federal savings associations, as well as federal branches and agencies of foreign banks.

State-chartered banks can also be supervised by the banking regulator in the state where they are chartered.

This is one reason two banks offering similar accounts may not have exactly the same supervisory structure.

4. What Does Deposit Insurance Actually Mean?

Federal deposit insurance was designed to protect eligible deposits when an insured depository institution fails.

Coverage is not simply "$250,000 for everything a person owns." The standard FDIC insurance amount is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category. Different ownership arrangements can affect how coverage is calculated.

Likewise, the NCUA's Share Insurance Fund provides federal insurance for qualifying accounts at federally insured credit unions under its own coverage rules.

Very important: Stocks, bonds, mutual funds and crypto assets are not FDIC-insured deposits merely because they may be purchased through or associated with a banking organization.

5. What Happens If a Bank Fails?

A bank failure does not mean that every insured depositor simply loses the money in an insured account. The FDIC has procedures for resolving failed insured banks and protecting insured depositors.

A common resolution method involves another institution assuming deposits of the failed bank. The exact resolution depends on the circumstances of the failure.

This is one reason knowing whether your institution is federally insured — and understanding the applicable coverage rules — matters.

Follow the Law

🏛️ Policy & Legislative Trail

Banking rules do not appear from nowhere. Congress can enact statutes, presidents can sign legislation, agencies can issue regulations under legal authority, courts can interpret laws, and regulators supervise institutions under those legal frameworks.

1863 — National Currency Act The legislation established the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency and a system of nationally chartered banks. President Abraham Lincoln signed the legislation on February 25, 1863.
1913 — Federal Reserve Act Congress established the Federal Reserve System, creating a new institutional framework for central banking in the United States.
1933 — Banking Act of 1933 Enacted June 16, 1933, the Banking Act of 1933 created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation and made major changes to banking law.
1970 — Federal Credit Union Share Insurance Congress created the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund, establishing federal insurance for qualifying member accounts at federally insured credit unions.
Today — Statutes + Regulations + Supervision Banking law now consists of many statutes and regulations administered and enforced through multiple federal and state authorities.

6. Who Was Involved in the Banking Act of 1933?

Historical legislation is rarely the work of only one person. For that reason, MoneyTrackTemplates will identify documented roles rather than simply declaring that one politician "created" a law.

The Banking Act of 1933 combined legislation associated with Senator Carter Glass and Representative Henry B. Steagall. The official House historical record identifies both men with H.R. 5661.

Federal Reserve History describes Glass as a major force behind the banking-reform legislation and Steagall, chairman of the House Banking and Currency Committee, as an important advocate of federal deposit insurance.

President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the measure into law on June 16, 1933.

7. A Law and a Regulation Are Not the Same Thing

This distinction is easy to miss.

A simplified research trail:

Congress passes legislation → the legislation becomes law → an agency may receive authority or instructions under that law → the agency may propose regulations → the public may have an opportunity to comment → final regulations may be published → regulators supervise and enforce applicable requirements.

For example, the OCC explains that regulations governing national banks and federal savings associations are found in Title 12 of the Code of Federal Regulations. Proposed OCC rules are published for public comment before finalization.

This means that researching a financial policy sometimes requires following more than the original act of Congress. The trail can continue through regulations, Federal Register notices, agency guidance, enforcement actions and court decisions.
