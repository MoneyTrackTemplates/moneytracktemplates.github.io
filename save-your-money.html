Save Your Money—But Who Saves the Banks?

Why household savings still matter, what financial independence really means, and what happens when the institutions holding our money—or the government itself—decide they need to spend less.

Research & drafting: OpenAI's ChatGPT
Editing & publication: MoneyTrackTemplates
Research reviewed: September 28, 2026

Save Something

Grandma kept cash in the house.

Her children were told to open savings accounts.

Their children were told to build emergency funds, contribute to retirement accounts, establish good credit and make their money “work for them.”

The advice changed. The principle didn't.

Save something.

Because eventually something will go wrong. The car will break down. The refrigerator will quit. A job will disappear. A medical bill will arrive. A roof will leak. A family member will need help. Something that wasn't in this month's budget will suddenly become the most important item in it.

The first purpose of savings isn't necessarily earning interest. It is creating distance between an unexpected problem and a financial emergency.

Savings is stored independence.

💵 The $400 Test

For millions of Americans, that independence remains fragile.

In the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System report on the economic well-being of U.S. households in 2025, 63 percent of adults said they could cover a hypothetical $400 emergency expense entirely with cash or its equivalent.

Another 12 percent of adults said they could not cover the expense by any means.

For a larger emergency, the numbers become even more revealing. 55 percent of adults reported having rainy-day savings sufficient to cover three months of expenses. Thirty percent said they could not cover three months of expenses through the methods examined by the survey.

That is why saving matters.

Not because a bank advertisement says it does.

Not because a financial expert says everyone needs an emergency fund of exactly X months.

Because having money available gives a household options.

Without savings, a $400 problem may become debt.

With savings, it may simply be an unpleasant Tuesday.

🏠 Must Savings Be in a Bank?

No.

People have stored wealth in different ways for thousands of years.

Someone may keep money in a savings account. Another may use a credit union. Someone else may maintain certificates of deposit, United States Treasury securities, retirement assets or other appropriate financial instruments.

And yes, some people keep physical cash at home.

A home safe doesn't pay interest. Cash can lose purchasing power through inflation. Physical money can be stolen, lost or destroyed, and cash sitting at home does not receive federal deposit insurance.

But it does perform one function extraordinarily well:

It is there.

That doesn't make keeping large quantities of cash at home the right decision for everyone. Nor does it make a bank account the right location for every dollar.

Know where your money is, why it is there, what risks it faces, and how quickly you can get to it when you need it.

Saving is not a particular financial product. Saving is the decision not to consume everything you earn.

🛒 Everybody Wants Your Money

Once money has been earned, an extraordinary number of institutions would prefer that you do something with it.

Retailers would like you to spend it. Credit-card companies may profit when you borrow. Investment companies would like you to invest. Banks would like deposits. Advertisers compete for the attention that influences spending. Governments collect taxes and decide how public resources will be allocated.

None of those facts proves wrongdoing.

They demonstrate something much more ordinary:

Everybody has incentives.

So does the saver.

The saver's incentive is sometimes remarkably simple:

Keep some of it.

That decision can be surprisingly powerful in an economy designed to make spending easy.

🚪 The Financial Power of “No”

Savings doesn't merely buy things.

It can buy the ability not to do things.

A person with a financial reserve may have more ability to leave an unsuitable job.

A household with savings may repair a car without immediately financing the repair.

A consumer may be able to wait rather than purchase something on expensive credit.

Someone confronted with a salesperson's supposedly once-in-a-lifetime opportunity can simply say:

No.

That is one reason financial independence should not be measured only by investment returns.

Money you haven't spent retains another kind of value: choice.

🏦 Households Are Told to Prepare. What About Banks?

Households are routinely advised to maintain emergency reserves because nobody can guarantee that tomorrow will proceed according to plan.

Banks understand risk too.

They maintain capital and liquidity. They are supervised and regulated. Deposits at qualifying institutions may be federally insured. The banking system also contains mechanisms intended to contain financial crises.

Yet American history contains repeated episodes in which financial institutions encountered problems serious enough to trigger extraordinary government intervention.

If households are expected to prepare for their financial emergencies, what should happen when a financial institution cannot withstand its own?

Before answering, however, we need to understand what the word “bailout” actually means.

🧾 What Is a Bank Bailout?

“Bailout” is a wonderfully convenient word.

Unfortunately, it can describe very different things.

Government might inject capital into an institution.

It might lend money against collateral.

It might guarantee certain obligations.

It might arrange the sale of a failing institution.

It might protect depositors while allowing shareholders to lose their investment.

It might acquire troubled assets.

Or Congress might establish an entirely new emergency program.

Those arrangements have different beneficiaries, risks and costs.

Whenever somebody says, “The government bailed them out,” ask:

Who was protected?
Who lost money?
Who supplied the money?
Was it a loan, purchase, guarantee or grant?
Was the money repaid?
What was the final cost?
Who ultimately absorbed it?

Now we're following the money.

📉 2008: The Troubled Asset Relief Program

The financial crisis of 2008 provides an excellent example of why the details matter.

Congress created the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) through the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008.

Congress originally authorized as much as $700 billion for the program. According to the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), $444 billion was ultimately disbursed.

That does not mean taxpayers permanently lost $444 billion.

The Congressional Budget Office's final accounting, published in 2024 after the last remaining Treasury investment had been repaid, estimated the program's total subsidy cost at $31 billion.

The Congressional Budget Office reported that much of the program's ultimate cost resulted from mortgage-foreclosure prevention programs, assistance to American International Group, and assistance to the automobile industry.

Taken together, other transactions involving financial institutions generated a net gain to the federal government through dividends, interest and capital gains.

So was TARP a bailout?

It certainly involved extraordinary federal intervention and substantial public financial risk.

But saying simply “the banks got $700 billion and taxpayers lost it” would not accurately describe the final accounting.

The receipts matter.

🏦 2023: When the Depositors Were Protected

The failures of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank in 2023 give us another useful example.

Normally, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) insures qualifying bank deposits up to statutory limits.

Regulators invoked a systemic-risk exception and protected all depositors at the two banks, including deposits above ordinary insurance limits.

Who was protected?

Depositors.

Shareholders and certain unsecured debtholders were not protected, and senior management was removed.

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation subsequently adopted a special assessment to recover losses to its Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF) associated with protecting uninsured depositors.

The assessment was structured primarily around larger banking organizations with substantial uninsured deposits.

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation's current special-assessment page notes that the estimated amount is adjusted as assets are sold, liabilities are satisfied and receivership expenses are incurred.

“The bank was rescued” and “the depositors were protected” are not necessarily the same statement.

If we want to debate fairness, we should first know which one actually happened.

🛡️ Who Saves the Saver?

People place money in banks partly because banks can provide security, payment services, convenience and federal deposit insurance.

The banking system, meanwhile, depends upon public confidence.

If depositors believe their money is unsafe, they can withdraw it.

If enough depositors do that simultaneously, a problem that begins at one institution can spread.

That helps explain why governments sometimes intervene during financial crises.

Policymakers may be concerned not merely with the institution that made poor decisions, but with depositors, businesses, payrolls, credit markets and other financial institutions that could be affected by a cascading failure.

That explanation does not automatically justify every intervention.

When government assumes financial risk to stabilize a system, who receives the benefit—and who ultimately pays for it?

🏛️ And Then the Government Decided It Needed to Save Money

Households aren't the only ones told to cut expenses.

Beginning in 2025, the federal government undertook a major effort to reduce spending and shrink parts of the federal workforce.

The Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE) was established to pursue changes in federal operations and spending.

Federal agencies reduced their workforces through several mechanisms, including hiring restrictions, voluntary resignations or retirement incentives, and reductions in force (RIFs)—a formal federal process through which positions or employees may be eliminated under applicable rules.

The scale was substantial.

The United States Government Accountability Office (GAO)— an independent legislative-branch agency that audits and evaluates federal programs for Congress—examined workforce data from 22 major federal agencies.

During 2025, nearly 378,000 employees separated from those agencies while approximately 127,000 employees were hired.

Between December 2024 and January 2026, their combined workforce declined by nearly 256,000 employees, or more than 11 percent.

Those aren't estimates of internet chatter. Those are government workforce numbers.

📋 Some Workers Were Paid to Leave

One particularly unusual mechanism was the Deferred Resignation Program (DRP).

The United States Office of Personnel Management (OPM)— the federal agency responsible for major aspects of federal civilian personnel administration—introduced the program in 2025.

Under the program, participating employees agreed to separate from federal service while generally being placed on paid administrative leave until their resignation or retirement date under the program.

In other words, reducing payroll eventually required spending money first.

And quite a lot of it.

💰 It Cost Money to Save Money

In September 2026, the Government Accountability Office reported that the federal agencies in its review spent an estimated $9.5 billion in salary costs associated with paid administrative leave in 2025.

That was approximately six times the amount spent in 2023.

The Government Accountability Office estimated that approximately $6.7 billion of the 2025 amount was associated with the Deferred Resignation Program.

The auditors also warned that problems in the underlying leave data could overstate the actual amount.

The Office of Personnel Management argued that the Deferred Resignation Program would produce recurring annual savings, but the Government Accountability Office concluded that the government's existing reporting system did not allow the costs of workforce-reduction leave to be tracked easily and accurately enough to determine reliably whether government-wide cost-saving goals were being met.

If spending billions today is supposed to save additional billions tomorrow, how will we know whether it worked?

That requires more than announcing a workforce reduction.

We need the ledger.

🧮 A Claimed Saving Is Not Yet a Saved Dollar

This principle applies to households, businesses and governments alike.

Suppose you cancel a $100 monthly service.

You might say: “I saved $1,200 this year.”

But if canceling that service requires paying a $500 termination fee and purchasing a $400 replacement, the accounting becomes more complicated.

Government accounting is vastly more complicated still.

A position eliminated from one agency might disappear entirely.

Or some of the work might stop.

Or remaining employees might absorb it.

Or a contractor might eventually perform it.

Or another agency might assume it.

Or the reduction might create future costs that are difficult to identify immediately.

Those possibilities don't prove that workforce reductions save money or lose money.

They explain why the net result has to be measured.

🧾 The DOGE “Wall of Receipts”

The Department of Government Efficiency created a public webpage called the Wall of Receipts, reporting estimated savings from terminated contracts, grants and real-estate leases.

As of July 7, 2026, the Wall of Receipts reported approximately $110 billion in savings across those three categories.

Then the Government Accountability Office examined the receipts.

Its conclusion was considerably more complicated.

The Government Accountability Office found that some estimates were incorrect or lacked adequate supporting evidence.

For grants, auditors said DOGE had not provided enough information to verify the calculation method behind 96 percent of the reported grant savings.

The auditors also found that 108 of the 264 leases DOGE identified for termination had already been in the process of termination before DOGE was established.

That does not demonstrate that no money was saved. It demonstrates why the word “savings” deserves the same scrutiny when government uses it that it deserves when a bank or corporation uses it.

🔎 So Where Did the Money Go?

Now we arrive at the question people should ask more often.

Suppose the government eliminates a position that would otherwise have cost $100,000 next year.

There isn't necessarily a $100,000 pile of cash sitting somewhere with a tag reading:

SAVED MONEY — DO NOT TOUCH.

Federal budgeting doesn't work that way.

Depending on the particular appropriation, legislation, account and timing, reduced spending may mean less money is obligated or spent than otherwise expected.

The budgetary effect may ultimately reduce an agency's expenditures, affect available balances, reduce future appropriations, or reduce the amount the government must finance through borrowing.

“We eliminated $10 billion of planned spending” is not automatically equivalent to “the federal government now has $10 billion in cash to spend somewhere else.”

Nor does a spending reduction by itself tell us what subsequently happened to federal deficits or debt.

To determine that, researchers must follow appropriations, outlays, revenues and borrowing.

There is no substitute for the accounting trail.

↔️ Saving Money and Moving Money Are Not the Same Thing

Reducing one expenditure does not tell you what happened to the money afterward.

Congress can change taxes.

Congress can increase or decrease other spending.

Agencies can operate under different appropriations.

Interest costs can change.

Revenues can rise or fall.

Emergency spending can occur.

A government can reduce one program substantially while the overall deficit still increases because other parts of the budget changed at the same time.

That is why public claims such as “We saved taxpayers $X billion” should always produce another question:

Compared with what?

And then another:

Did total government spending actually decline by that amount?

And another:

What happened to borrowing?

And eventually:

Who gained and who lost from the complete package of changes?

Those aren't partisan questions.

They're accounting questions.

📊 What Does Greed Look Like in a Ledger?

“Greed” is an emotionally powerful word.

It is also difficult to measure.

A corporation seeking profit is behaving according to one of its basic economic incentives.

A household trying to keep more of its income has an incentive.

A bank trying to minimize its funding costs has an incentive.

A government official trying to reduce expenditures has an incentive.

An organization seeking a larger appropriation has an incentive.

A taxpayer seeking lower taxes has an incentive.

None of those facts alone establishes greed.

So rather than attempting to measure somebody's heart, measure something we can observe.

Who received the money? → How much? → What was promised? → What happened? → Who assumed the risk? → Who received the upside? → Who absorbed the loss?

Was the claimed saving independently verified?

Did the policy cost something elsewhere?

Who was better or worse off afterward?

If readers examine those facts and conclude that an arrangement looks greedy, generous, prudent, wasteful, necessary or unfair, they will at least be forming that judgment from evidence.

👀 Keep an Eye on “Big Brother”

George Orwell gave popular culture a wonderfully memorable phrase: Big Brother.

We can borrow it here with a smile.

Keeping an eye on government doesn't require assuming government is evil.

It requires remembering that government handles enormous amounts of other people's money.

That deserves attention.

Government reports are public for a reason.

Budgets are worth reading.

Audits are worth reading.

Legislation is worth reading.

Inspector-general reports are worth reading.

Government Accountability Office reports are especially worth reading.

And when one government office says it saved billions while another government agency says some of those savings cannot be independently verified from the available information, citizens do not have to choose a team.

They can read both reports.

That is financial literacy too.

🕵️ Watch the Watchers—and Watch the Money

Here is a useful checklist whenever a government, corporation, bank or other institution announces a major savings initiative.

Question What the researcher is looking for
What was the original expense? Establish the baseline.
What exactly was eliminated? A contract, employee, grant, lease, program or something else?
What did eliminating it cost? Severance, paid leave, termination charges, transition expenses or other costs.
Was the saving actual or projected? Money not yet spent is different from cash recovered.
Who performed the work afterward? Nobody, remaining employees, another agency or a contractor?
Was the saving independently verified? If so, by whom?
What happened to total spending? Did the institution actually spend less?
What happened afterward? Did the change affect borrowing, available balances or later budgets?
Who benefited? Identify the beneficiaries.
Who absorbed the cost? Identify the final bearer of the loss or expense.
That's how you follow the money without having to follow anybody's political slogan.

🏛️ Saving Is More Than Personal Finance

At first glance, a household emergency fund and a federal audit don't seem to have much in common.

They do.

Both are about resources and choices.

A household decides whether to consume money today or preserve some for tomorrow.

A bank decides how to fund itself, price deposits, lend and manage risk.

A corporation decides where costs can be reduced and profits increased.

A government decides what to tax, what to spend, what to borrow and what to eliminate.

In every case, money represents choices that can no longer be made once the money is gone.

That's why saving matters.

And that's why watching what institutions do with money matters too.

🔐 The Quiet Rebellion of Keeping Something

Modern commerce has become extraordinarily good at removing friction from spending.

One click → Tap to pay → Buy now, pay later → Automatic renewal → Instant delivery → Personalized advertising → Subscription billing

The easier spending becomes, the more deliberate saving has to become.

There is nothing glamorous about moving money into an emergency reserve.

Nobody applauds.

No package arrives tomorrow.

No advertisement congratulates you.

But you have purchased something nonetheless.

Future choice.

Perhaps that is why saving can feel almost rebellious in a culture constantly inviting us to consume.

You earned it.

You don't necessarily have to spend it.

🎓 For Young Researchers: Know the Agencies

Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System

The governing body of the Federal Reserve System, the central banking system of the United States. Among other responsibilities, the Federal Reserve conducts monetary policy, supervises and regulates certain financial institutions, and works to promote financial-system stability.

Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)

An independent federal agency that insures qualifying deposits at insured banks and savings associations and plays a major role in resolving failed insured institutions.

United States Government Accountability Office (GAO)

An independent agency in the legislative branch that performs audits, investigations and evaluations for Congress. It is sometimes described as the congressional watchdog.

United States Office of Personnel Management (OPM)

The federal government's principal civilian human-resources agency. It administers and oversees many federal workforce policies and programs.

Congressional Budget Office (CBO)

A nonpartisan legislative-branch agency that provides Congress with budgetary and economic analysis.

United States Department of the Treasury

The executive department responsible for areas including federal finances, government borrowing, financial policy and administration of the public debt.

Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE)

An initiative established in 2025 to pursue changes in federal operations and spending. Despite its name, researchers should distinguish it from a traditional Cabinet department established by statute.

Research Tip

When one of these organizations publishes a number, identify which organization produced it.

Government is not a single speaker. Different agencies perform different jobs, use different datasets and sometimes examine one another's claims.

📚 Public Administration Research Note

This subject extends well beyond personal finance.

For students of Public Administration, it raises questions about governmental accountability, administrative capacity, workforce management, program evaluation, budgeting, regulatory policy and institutional trust.

The 2025–2026 federal workforce changes offer an especially useful research problem.

Questions for Further Research

Did reductions in personnel produce the projected savings?

What were the transition costs?

Which agencies experienced the largest reductions?

Were services reduced?

Were responsibilities transferred to contractors or other organizations?

How should savings be measured?

How long must researchers wait before a short-term expense can be compared fairly with a claimed long-term saving?

What evidence would prove that the policy succeeded or failed?

Those are empirical questions.

They deserve empirical answers.

Bottom Line

Saving money is an act of preparation.

It doesn't require predicting the next recession, bank failure, layoff or broken refrigerator.

It requires accepting that eventually something unexpected will happen.

Save something.
Know where your money is.
Understand who holds it.
Understand what protects it.
Question the incentives.
Check the claims.
Read the audits.
Watch the watchers.
Check the receipts.

And when somebody—whether a bank, business or government—tells you how much money has been saved, ask one final question:

Where did the money go?

Then follow it.
