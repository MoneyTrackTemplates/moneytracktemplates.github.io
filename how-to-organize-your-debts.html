How to Organize Your Debts Before Paying Them Off

A beginner-friendly guide to understanding balances, minimum payments, interest rates, due dates, payoff strategies, and Excel debt tracking.

Having several debts can feel confusing before you even begin paying them down.

One bill may show a balance. Another lists an interest rate. A credit card statement may show a minimum payment, due date, and available credit. A student loan account may contain several individual loan balances.

Then someone tells you to create a debt payoff plan.

Where do you even start?

Start by getting organized.

Before comparing payoff strategies or deciding where extra money should go, create one clear list showing what you owe.

You do not need to be a financial expert or an Excel expert to do this.

Your first goal: Know what you owe, whom you owe, how much you are required to pay, and when each payment is due.

What Is Debt?

Debt is money you owe to another person, company, lender, or organization.

Common examples include:

  • Credit cards
  • Student loans
  • Auto loans
  • Personal loans
  • Medical bills
  • Buy-now-pay-later balances
  • Other installment loans

Some debts charge interest. Some may charge fees. Different debts can also have different payment schedules and rules.

That is why simply knowing your total debt isn't always enough. You also need to understand the individual debts that make up that total.

If You're a Student, This Article Can Still Be Useful

You may be reading this before you have any debt at all. That's actually a good time to learn these concepts.

Middle- and high-school students can learn what borrowing, interest, balances, and payments mean before making major financial decisions later.

College and graduate students may encounter:

  • Student loans
  • Credit cards
  • Car loans
  • Medical bills
  • School-related payment plans
  • Other forms of borrowing

You don't need to have debt to learn how debt works. Understanding the vocabulary now can make financial information much less intimidating later.

A Few Debt Words You Should Know

Balance

Your balance is the amount currently owed on a debt.

Minimum Payment

A minimum payment is the minimum amount the lender requires you to pay for that billing period.

Interest

Interest is generally the cost of borrowing money.

Interest Rate

The interest rate helps determine how much interest is charged.

APR

APR stands for Annual Percentage Rate. It is commonly used to express the yearly cost of borrowing, although how APR works can depend on the financial product.

Due Date

The due date is the date by which a payment is due.

Lender or Creditor

A lender or creditor is the company, financial institution, organization, or other party to which money is owed.

Principal

The principal generally refers to the amount borrowed before interest and certain other charges.

Step 1: Gather Your Debt Information

Before making a payoff plan, gather information about each debt.

You might find this information on:

  • Monthly statements
  • Online account pages
  • Loan documents
  • Billing statements
  • Account summaries
  • Other lender or creditor records

For each debt, try to identify:

  • Name of lender or creditor
  • Type of debt
  • Current balance
  • Minimum payment
  • Interest rate or APR, when applicable
  • Payment due date
  • Account status
  • Other information you need for your records

You are not making a payoff decision yet. You're simply gathering the facts.

Step 6: Understand Why Interest Rates Matter

Imagine two hypothetical debts:

Debt A: $1,000 balance at 5%

Debt B: $1,000 balance at 25%

Both have the same balance, but their interest rates are very different.

You do not need to calculate complicated interest formulas just to get organized. At this stage, record the interest rate accurately so you have the information available when comparing payoff approaches.

Step 7: Debt Snowball vs. Debt Avalanche

Debt Snowball

With the debt snowball approach, debts are generally prioritized from the smallest balance to the largest balance, while required payments continue on other debts.

  1. $600 debt
  2. $1,200 debt
  3. $5,000 debt
  4. $8,000 debt

Some people like this approach because eliminating a smaller balance can provide a visible sense of progress.

Debt Avalanche

With the debt avalanche approach, debts are generally prioritized by interest rate, beginning with the highest rate, while required payments continue on other debts.

  1. 24%
  2. 18%
  3. 7%
  4. 5%

Depending on the debts and payment assumptions, this approach can reduce total interest compared with other ordering strategies.

Which method should you use?

There is not one universal answer for every person. Interest rates, balances, loan terms, income, expenses, available cash, and individual priorities can all matter. This guide is designed to help you organize the information so you can understand and compare different approaches.

Excel Beginner Corner

A debt tracker is simply information organized into rows and columns.

Workbook: The entire Excel file.

Worksheet: A page or tab inside the workbook.

Cell: One individual box in the spreadsheet.

Row: A horizontal line of cells.

Column: A vertical line of cells.

You do not need to become an Excel expert before using a debt-tracking spreadsheet.

How to Create a Simple Debt List in Excel

If you're starting with a blank worksheet, create headings such as:

Debt Name Current Balance Minimum Payment Interest Rate Due Date

Then enter one debt on each row.

Debt Name Current Balance Minimum Payment Interest Rate Due Date Card A $1,200 $45 24% 8th Card B $600 $30 18% 15th Student Loan $5,000 $75 5% 21st

That's already a basic debt tracker. You can make it more sophisticated later.

Excel Beginner Tip: Protect Formula Cells

A pre-built Excel debt tracker may contain formulas that calculate totals, payment amounts, payoff estimates, or other information automatically.

Beginner Rule:

If a cell contains something that begins with an equals sign, such as =SUM(B2:B10), it probably contains a formula. Avoid erasing it unless you understand what that cell is designed to do.

How to Save Your Debt Tracker

Consider keeping an untouched master version of your tracker.

MASTER - Debt Tracker.xlsx

Then create your working version using:

File → Save As

For example:

My Debt Tracker - 2026.xlsx

Your clean master copy remains available if you ever need to start over.

Do You Need a New Debt Tracker Every Month?

Not necessarily.

A debt tracker may continue over many months so that you can maintain a payment history and watch your balances change.

A workbook might include tabs such as:

Debt Overview Payment Log Payoff Plan Dashboard

You can continue updating the same working workbook as payments are made.

Back Up Your Debt Tracker

If you have been updating a workbook for several months, consider keeping periodic backup copies.

Be Careful What You Put in a Spreadsheet

A debt tracker generally does not need:
  • Online banking passwords
  • Credit-card PINs
  • Full credit-card numbers
  • Account passwords
  • Social Security numbers
  • Security-question answers
Use simple labels such as Credit Card A, Car Loan, or Student Loan 1.

What feels like one student loan may sometimes consist of multiple individual loans with different balances or interest rates.

If your account shows separate loans, recording each one separately may give you a clearer picture.

What If You Still Use a Credit Card You're Paying Down?

Credit-card balances can change even after you make a payment.

Interest, new purchases, credits, fees, and payment timing can all affect the balance.

Tracking Tip: Update the balance using current account information instead of assuming that Old Balance − Payment always equals the new balance.

What If You Don't Know an Interest Rate?

Don't guess.

Check your statement, lender account, loan documents, or other official records.

If you cannot determine it immediately, leave the field blank temporarily and return to it later.

A blank cell is better than knowingly entering incorrect information.

What If You Miss a Payment?

Do not change your spreadsheet to make it look as though the payment happened.

Record what actually happened and check the account or contact the lender or creditor if you need current information about the amount due or available options.

A financial tracker is most useful when it reflects reality.

Common Debt-Tracking Mistakes

Keeping Everything in Your Head

Writing balances, rates, and due dates down gives you one place to review everything.

Tracking Only the Total Balance

Knowing the total amount is useful, but knowing which debts make up that total is much more informative.

Forgetting Interest Rates

Interest rates can matter when comparing debts and payoff approaches.

Forgetting Due Dates

A payoff strategy does not replace the need to keep track of required payments and due dates.

Assuming Every Balance Changes by the Payment Amount

Interest, fees, new charges, credits, and timing can affect actual balances.

Deleting Spreadsheet Formulas

Changing formula cells can cause automatic calculations to stop working.

Storing Sensitive Account Information

Your tracker does not need banking passwords or full account credentials.

Review Your Debt Tracker Regularly

When you make a payment: Record it.

When a new statement arrives: Check the current balance and important account information.

Once a month: Review all debts together.

Periodically: Save a backup copy of your workbook.

What Happens When You Pay Off a Debt?

Consider keeping the completed debt in your records instead of immediately deleting it.

You can add a Status column and mark it Paid Off.

Credit Card B $600 $0 Paid Off
Student Loan $5,000 $3,850 Active
Car Loan $8,000 $6,400 Active

Keeping completed debts visible can help preserve your history and show how far you've come.

Organization Comes Before the Payoff Strategy

When people want to get rid of debt, it is natural to immediately ask:

Which debt should I pay first?

But an important step comes before that.

Know exactly what you're working with.

Gather your balances. Record your minimum payments. Find your interest rates. Write down your due dates. Calculate your totals. Understand how those payments fit into your budget.

Then you have a much clearer foundation for evaluating different approaches.

You do not need to solve everything in one afternoon. Start by organizing the information.

That's progress too.
