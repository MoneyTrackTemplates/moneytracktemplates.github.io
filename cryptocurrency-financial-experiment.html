Cryptocurrency: Money, Investment—or the World's Largest Financial Experiment?

You bought something called a coin. There is no coin. The bank is gone. Your password is now part of your retirement plan.

MoneyTrackTemplates · October 2026

Kevin has decided to become an investor.

This decision occurred at approximately 9:17 p.m. on Tuesday after a coworker named Derek announced that he'd made $18,000 trading cryptocurrency.

Kevin knows absolutely nothing about cryptocurrency.

This presents no apparent obstacle.

By 9:32, Kevin has watched three videos.

By 9:46, he has opened an account.

At 10:03, Kevin purchases $5,000 worth of cryptocurrency.

At 10:11, his investment rises 7%.

Kevin changes his social-media biography to:

Investor | Entrepreneur | Digital Assets

Kevin begins wondering why everybody doesn't do this.

The following morning, his investment is down 31%.

Kevin removes "Digital Assets" from his biography and begins researching long-term fundamentals.

Welcome to cryptocurrency.

1. First Question: What Did Kevin Actually Buy?

This is where cryptocurrency immediately becomes peculiar.

Kevin bought something called a coin.

There is no coin.

There isn't even one of those disappointing commemorative coins that arrives in a velvet box after midnight television.

What Kevin owns is a digital asset recorded through a cryptographically secured distributed ledger, commonly a blockchain.

Bitcoin, introduced in 2009, demonstrated that people could transfer a scarce digital asset without requiring a traditional bank to maintain the central ledger.

That was genuinely innovative.

A network of computers could collectively maintain transaction records according to predetermined rules.

No Bank of Kevin.

No Federal Reserve of Kevin.

No teller asking Kevin whether he'd like to open a savings account while he's standing there holding his paycheck.

But cryptocurrency eventually expanded far beyond Bitcoin.

Today the category includes thousands of digital assets, stablecoins, utility tokens, governance tokens, decentralized-finance projects and meme coins.

Some have serious technological objectives.

Some are speculative investments.

And some appear to have been created because somebody discovered that investors respond favorably to cartoon dogs.

Those distinctions matter.

Calling all cryptocurrency the same thing is rather like calling a Treasury bond, a casino chip, a dollar bill and a Beanie Baby the same financial instrument because all four can theoretically be exchanged for money.

2. Why Would Anybody Want This?

Before we begin sharpening the knives, cryptocurrency deserves a fair hearing.

There are legitimate reasons people find the technology attractive.

Digital assets can be transferred across borders. Some blockchain networks operate continuously rather than closing for weekends and banking holidays. People can hold certain digital assets without maintaining a conventional bank account.

Distributed-ledger technology may also have useful applications beyond speculative cryptocurrency.

And then there is Bitcoin's central appeal.

Its supply rules aren't determined by a government.

For people worried about inflation, currency instability, capital controls or government monetary policy, that can be attractive.

Some investors also bought Bitcoin years ago, held it, and made enormous legitimate gains.

Those people exist.

Kevin has met several of them on the internet.

Curiously, he has encountered considerably fewer people announcing:

"Hello. I bought at the top and lost $38,000."

Investment conversations have a peculiar survivor bias.

The yacht photographs tend to circulate more widely than the bankruptcy documents.

3. Congratulations. You Have Escaped the Bank.

One of cryptocurrency's great promises was removing financial intermediaries.

Why should a bank stand between two people transferring value?

Fair question.

With self-custody, cryptocurrency can allow Kevin to control his assets using cryptographic keys.

Kevin is thrilled.

Kevin: "So the bank can't freeze my account?"

Potentially, yes.

Kevin: "Nobody controls my password?"

Correct.

Kevin: "What happens if I lose it?"

Ah.

Welcome to banking, Kevin. You are the bank.

The bank's cybersecurity department is Kevin.

The fraud department is Kevin.

The password-recovery department is Kevin.

The employee responsible for not sending $17,000 to the wrong digital address is also Kevin.

Kevin suddenly misses the woman at the credit union.

Removing an intermediary can remove some costs and restrictions.

It can also remove some of the protections and services the intermediary provided.

Decentralization doesn't eliminate responsibility.

It relocates it.

Usually directly onto Kevin.

4. So Kevin Uses a Cryptocurrency Exchange

Kevin decides self-custody sounds exhausting.

He therefore leaves his assets with a cryptocurrency exchange.

Problem solved.

Except we have now recreated an intermediary.

This is where our story meets Sam Bankman-Fried.

Bankman-Fried founded FTX, once one of the world's prominent cryptocurrency exchanges.

Federal prosecutors proved that Bankman-Fried misappropriated billions of dollars belonging to FTX customers and funneled customer deposits to his affiliated trading firm, Alameda Research.

He was convicted on seven fraud and conspiracy counts.

In March 2024, he was sentenced to 25 years in federal prison and ordered to forfeit more than $11 billion.

An important distinction: Bitcoin did not steal FTX customers' money. A cryptocurrency did not commit the fraud. People running a cryptocurrency business did.

The underlying technology didn't commit fraud.

Humans discovered that twenty-first-century financial technology remains remarkably compatible with one of humanity's oldest technologies:

Lying.

5. Escape the Banks! Give Us Your Money Instead.

Then we meet Alexander Mashinsky and Celsius.

Celsius marketed cryptocurrency financial services while promoting the idea that customers could "unbank" themselves.

Which is a marvelous word.

The traditional bank was apparently obsolete.

So customers transferred their assets to...

another company holding their assets.

Progress!

Federal prosecutors said Mashinsky misrepresented Celsius's financial condition and manipulated the market for its CEL token. He pleaded guilty to commodities and securities fraud.

When Celsius halted customer withdrawals in June 2022, hundreds of thousands of customers had approximately $4.7 billion in assets inaccessible on the platform.

Mashinsky was sentenced in May 2025 to 12 years in prison.

UNBANK YOURSELF!

Stop trusting traditional financial institutions.

Instead, trust us.

We are a financial institution with a cooler website.

There is a serious lesson beneath the joke.

A decentralized asset can still be held through a highly centralized business.

Once customers surrender custody, their risk is no longer limited to the cryptocurrency.

They must also evaluate the people holding it.

6. But Surely Nobody Invented a Fake Cryptocurrency?

Allow us to introduce OneCoin.

OneCoin was promoted internationally as a cryptocurrency through a massive multilevel-marketing network.

According to the United States Department of Justice, victims invested more than $4 billion worldwide.

There was just one rather awkward problem with the revolutionary cryptocurrency.

The government describes OneCoin as fraudulent.

Its co-founder, Ruja Ignatova, disappeared and remains a fugitive.

Investor: "Excellent presentation. May I examine the blockchain?"

Promoter: "No."

Investor: "Where can I independently verify transactions?"

Promoter: "You're focusing on negativity."

Investor: "How is the price determined?"

Promoter: "Have you considered becoming a Diamond Executive?"

Apparently, the future of finance occasionally requires fewer questions.

7. And Then Someone Invented a Magical Trading Robot

Our tour would be incomplete without BitConnect.

BitConnect promoters told investors about proprietary technology known as the BitConnect Trading Bot and "Volatility Software," supposedly capable of producing substantial profits and guaranteed returns.

Guaranteed.

Returns.

Two words that should cause every investor's eyebrow to rise approximately four inches.

Federal prosecutors described BitConnect as a massive cryptocurrency investment scheme. Top U.S. promoter Glenn Arcaro pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit wire fraud.

In 2023, a federal court ordered more than $17 million in restitution distributed to approximately 800 victims in more than 40 countries.

If an investment contains the phrases "guaranteed returns," "secret algorithm," "proprietary trading bot" and "you must act today," Kevin will act today.

He will leave.

Kevin is learning.

8. Is Cryptocurrency Itself a Scam?

No.

And that distinction matters.

Fraud involving cryptocurrency does not establish that cryptocurrency itself is fraudulent.

Criminals also use dollars.

Nobody arrests the twenty-dollar bill.

But cryptocurrency has characteristics that can be extremely useful to scammers: rapid transfers, global reach, technological complexity, transactions that may be difficult or impossible to reverse, and an investing public that may not understand what it is purchasing.

The Federal Trade Commission reported approximately $1.42 billion in reported fraud losses paid through cryptocurrency during 2024.

That's enormous.

But reported fraud losses involving bank transfers and payments were even larger—approximately $2.09 billion that year.

So the evidence does not say:

Cryptocurrency = fraud.

It says:

Wherever people move large amounts of money, criminals eventually arrive carrying PowerPoint presentations.

9. Kevin Discovers Stablecoins

After his volatile cryptocurrency adventure, Kevin wants something safer.

He discovers stablecoins.

Unlike Bitcoin, a dollar-pegged stablecoin generally aims to maintain a value around one U.S. dollar.

Kevin is fascinated.

Finally!

Digital money without all that troublesome dollar business.

He purchases $1,000 worth of dollar-denominated stablecoins.

They are worth approximately...

$1,000.

Kevin has successfully escaped the dollar.

By purchasing something designed to imitate the dollar.

Federal Reserve officials have described dollar stablecoins as essentially synthetic dollars. Dollar-denominated stablecoins represent the overwhelming majority of the stablecoin market.

This is where our story becomes deliciously strange.

Cryptocurrency emerged partly from dissatisfaction with conventional monetary systems.

Yet much of the cryptocurrency marketplace conducts business using digital instruments deliberately attached to the U.S. dollar.

Kevin has escaped fiat currency.

The dollar has followed him.

10. Is Cryptocurrency Destroying the Dollar?

This is where we should be particularly careful.

There is no good evidence that cryptocurrency has simply destroyed or replaced the U.S. dollar.

Bitcoin can compete with conventional money for certain functions. Some investors treat it as a store of value. Cryptocurrency can also facilitate transactions that don't require traditional dollar-payment rails.

But dollar stablecoins may actually extend the dollar's international reach.

A person living somewhere with an unstable local currency may not have easy access to an American bank account.

But that person may have a smartphone.

A dollar-backed digital token potentially provides another route to dollar exposure.

Crypto was supposed to challenge traditional money.

Instead, one branch of crypto may be distributing digital dollars around the planet.

The revolution has apparently acquired a U.S. dollar franchise.

11. The Dollarization Problem

That doesn't mean stablecoins are harmless.

Suppose residents of a country stop trusting their local currency.

They begin saving and conducting business using dollar-denominated stablecoins instead.

For the individual, that might appear rational.

For the country's central bank, however, this could become a serious problem.

If residents increasingly abandon the domestic currency for digital dollars, the local central bank may have less influence over monetary conditions.

This resembles traditional dollarization, except people no longer necessarily need suitcases full of American banknotes.

They need phones.

The potential consequence is almost backwards from the popular fear.

Cryptocurrency might not weaken the U.S. dollar first.

Dollar-based cryptocurrency could potentially weaken other currencies while expanding dollar usage.

Kevin stares at his stablecoin.

This was not in the brochure.

12. And Guess What's Behind Some of Those Digital Dollars?

Stablecoins require us to ask a wonderfully old-fashioned question:

What's backing them?

Depending on the stablecoin and its structure, reserves may include cash and highly liquid financial assets.

That can include short-term U.S. Treasury securities.

Which means our revolutionary digital financial ecosystem can ultimately lead us back to...

the United States Treasury.

Remember our previous investigation, Who Holds America's Debt?

Well, look who's back.

Kevin wanted freedom from traditional finance.

Kevin bought cryptocurrency.

Kevin moved into stablecoins.

The stablecoin system relies substantially on dollars and reserve assets.

Some reserve structures include Treasury securities.

Kevin has completed an extraordinary financial journey.

He left Washington...

circled the blockchain...

passed through cyberspace...

and arrived back at the Treasury market.

13. The Government Would Also Like a Word

Kevin has one final misconception.

He believes cryptocurrency exists outside the government's tax system.

The Internal Revenue Service would like Kevin to sit down.

The IRS treats digital assets as property for federal income-tax purposes.

Selling, exchanging or otherwise disposing of digital assets can create reportable gains or losses.

Digital-asset broker reporting has also expanded through Form 1099-DA, Digital Asset Proceeds From Broker Transactions.

Kevin looks wounded.

He thought he had escaped government money.

He has instead acquired government paperwork.

14. So What Exactly Have We Invented?

After several weeks of research, Kevin has learned something important.

Cryptocurrency isn't one thing.

Bitcoin isn't FTX.

FTX isn't a stablecoin.

A stablecoin isn't OneCoin.

Blockchain technology isn't Sam Bankman-Fried.

And a criminal using cryptocurrency doesn't prove that the technology itself is criminal.

There are legitimate technological innovations here.

There are legitimate investors.

There are legitimate payment applications.

There are also speculative bubbles, dubious tokens, collapsed businesses, breathtaking frauds and enough financial jargon to render an otherwise sensible adult temporarily defenseless.

That's why the most useful question isn't:

"Is crypto good or bad?"

That's far too easy.

Better questions are:

What exactly am I buying?

Who controls it?

Who holds it?

What gives it value?

Can I sell it?

Who promises to redeem it?

What happens if that company disappears?

What happens if I lose my credentials?

What laws apply?

What taxes apply?

What protects me?

And where is the money actually coming from?

If the person selling the investment becomes irritated by those questions, Kevin now understands something.

Those questions were probably overdue.

15. Kevin Becomes a Financial Conservative

Kevin has not abandoned cryptocurrency.

That's important.

He has simply stopped believing that the word blockchain suspends the laws of mathematics, economics and human behavior.

He understands that an asset can contain interesting technology and still be overpriced.

A legitimate industry can attract criminals.

A decentralized network can lead users straight into a centralized exchange.

An investment can produce extraordinary returns and extraordinary losses.

And an invention designed partly to escape conventional finance can end up depending heavily on the world's dominant conventional currency.

Kevin looks at his portfolio.

Bitcoin.

Stablecoins.

Dollars.

A Treasury fund.

A savings account.

Kevin smiles.

His social-media biography now says:

"I have no idea what happens next."

Finally.

Kevin has become an experienced investor.

The Great Cryptocurrency Paradox

Cryptocurrency promised a world in which people could transfer value without traditional banks.

It delivered that technology.

Then people built exchanges.

Cryptocurrency promised an alternative to government currencies.

Then the crypto economy adopted dollar-pegged stablecoins.

Cryptocurrency promised transparency through blockchains.

Then some customers handed billions of dollars to companies whose internal finances they could not see.

Cryptocurrency promised financial independence.

Then the Internal Revenue Service introduced digital-asset reporting forms.

None of this proves cryptocurrency is destined to fail.

It proves something considerably more interesting.

Technology can reinvent the machinery of finance without reinventing human nature.

Greed remains compatible with every operating system.

Fraud has successfully migrated to the cloud.

And after years of cryptocurrency innovation, the U.S. dollar hasn't quietly packed its belongings and left.

In parts of the crypto economy, it has simply changed clothes.

So perhaps the question isn't whether cryptocurrency will destroy the dollar.

Perhaps the better question is:

Did cryptocurrency escape the financial system—or did the financial system simply learn how to use cryptocurrency?

No political team required. Just the receipts. 🧾
