Your Credit Score: Who Is Judging Whom?

How credit scores are created, who supplies the information, who profits from the system—and what happens when an old debt comes knocking

Research & drafting: OpenAI's ChatGPT
Editing & publication: MoneyTrackTemplates
Research reviewed: September 28, 2026

You Have Been Assigned a Number

Somewhere in the American financial system, there are numbers attached to your name.

You probably know this.

You may even know approximately what yours is.

Perhaps your bank displays one beside your checking account. Perhaps an app sends a cheerful little message when it rises six points, as though your credit score has just made the honor roll.

There is only one problem.

You don't actually have one credit score.

Different scoring models exist. Different versions of those models exist. Different credit-reporting companies may have different information about you. And a score can change as the underlying information changes.

So the number displayed on Tuesday afternoon is not necessarily the number a mortgage lender, automobile lender or credit-card company will see later.

This raises a much more interesting question than whether 742 is better than 738:

Who made this number, what did they put into it, and who is using it to judge you?

There.

Now we have something worth investigating.

First: Your Credit Report and Your Credit Score Are Not the Same Thing

These terms are often tossed around as though they mean the same thing.

They don't.

A credit report contains information about your credit history— accounts, balances, payment histories and other information reported about your financial activity.

A credit score is a number calculated from information in a credit report. Its purpose is generally to help predict credit risk.

The distinction matters.

Think of the credit report as the ingredients.

The scoring model is the recipe.

The score is the cake.

And lenders are deciding whether they would like a slice.

Meet the Three Big Credit Bureaus

The three nationwide credit-reporting companies are:

Equifax

Experian

TransUnion

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) identifies these as the three nationwide consumer-reporting companies, although many other specialized consumer-reporting companies also exist. CFPB: Consumer Reporting Companies

These companies collect information concerning consumers and prepare reports used in financial and other legally permitted decisions.

They are private companies.

They are not branches of the federal government.

And there isn't necessarily one magnificent master file labeled YOU sitting in a government vault somewhere.

Different bureaus can possess different information because creditors do not necessarily report every account to every reporting company. In fact, creditors generally are not required to report information to the credit-reporting companies at all. CFPB: Why Some Debts May Not Appear

Already our tidy little number is becoming considerably less tidy.

Who Is the Customer?

This is where the arrangement becomes particularly interesting.

You are the person being described.

Your accounts create the history.

Your payments help create the data.

Your missed payments create data too—and those are considerably less shy.

But you aren't necessarily the customer in the transaction.

Businesses use consumer reports in making various decisions about consumers, subject to federal law governing when those reports may be obtained. The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) restricts access to consumer reports to legally permissible purposes. CFPB: Permissible Purposes

So the consumer occupies a rather peculiar position:

The file is about you.
The score evaluates you.
Someone else may be using it to make a decision about you.

That doesn't make the arrangement sinister.

It does make it worth understanding.

Then Who Makes the Score?

Credit bureaus and credit-scoring companies aren't necessarily the same thing.

One of the best-known scoring systems is the FICO Score, developed by FICO.

FICO says its scores are calculated from information in credit reports. For the general population, it describes five broad categories used in its scoring: payment history, amounts owed, length of credit history, new credit and credit mix. FICO also cautions that the importance of these categories can vary among individual credit profiles. FICO: What's in Your Credit Score?

Other credit-scoring models exist.

Which means the statement:

“My credit score is 742.”

may be missing some useful information.

Which score?

Which model?

Which credit bureau supplied the underlying information?

When was the score calculated?

What information was present at the time?

Suddenly our tidy three-digit number has acquired quite a social life.

What Goes Into the Number?

For a FICO Score, payment history is particularly important. FICO describes payment history as approximately 35% of its general scoring framework, followed by amounts owed at approximately 30%, length of credit history at 15%, new credit at 10%, and credit mix at 10%. Those percentages are general guides rather than guarantees of how any single person's score will behave. FICO: Credit Score Factors

Notice what the score is trying to accomplish.

It isn't grading your character.

It isn't measuring intelligence.

It doesn't know whether you're a good parent, a dependable employee or the neighbor who brings everybody's garbage cans in when the wind blows them down the street.

It is trying to predict credit risk from financial information.

A credit score is a financial prediction, not a personality test.

Apparently there is no scoring category for bringing soup to your sick neighbor.

Your Score Can Cost You Money

The information in your credit report can affect whether you qualify for a loan and how much you pay to borrow. The CFPB therefore recommends reviewing credit reports for errors, particularly before major borrowing decisions such as purchasing a home or automobile. CFPB: When to Review Your Credit Report

That means a score isn't merely decorative.

Two people seeking similar loans may be offered different terms.

And over a large loan extending many years, a seemingly modest difference in interest rates can become a very substantial difference in dollars.

Never stop at the percentage. Convert it into money.

An interest rate looks wonderfully civilized when written as 6.5%.

The dollars it extracts over thirty years are considerably more talkative.

What If the Information Is Wrong?

Now the polite little three-digit number becomes less charming.

Credit reports can contain errors.

An account might not belong to you.

A payment history could be inaccurate.

A balance could be wrong.

Identity theft could produce accounts you never opened.

Federal law gives consumers the right to dispute inaccurate information. The CFPB says consumer-reporting companies must investigate disputes without charging the consumer, and consumers can also dispute information with the company that supplied it. CFPB: Consumer Reporting Companies

That's considerably more useful than a motivational quotation about rebuilding your financial life.

It's a legal right.

You May Inspect the File

And you should.

Consumers can obtain their reports through AnnualCreditReport.com.

The CFPB says consumers can currently review their credit reports online for free once a week from each of the three nationwide credit-reporting companies. Checking your own report does not hurt your credit score. CFPB: Checking Your Own Credit Report

So inspect the file.

After all, everybody else seems terribly interested in it.

You might as well have a look yourself.

Now About That Credit Card You Didn't Pay...

Here is where American credit folklore becomes especially entertaining.

You've probably heard some version of this:

“If you don't pay a credit card, eventually the bank writes it off.”

That's true—in a particular accounting sense.

If minimum payments go unpaid for several months, a creditor may charge off the debt as a loss. The Federal Trade Commission says this may occur after roughly four to six months of missed minimum payments. FTC: How to Get Out of Debt

The phrase charge off sounds wonderfully final.

Closed.

Finished.

Gone.

Perhaps somebody rang a little bell in Accounting and sent the debt to its eternal rest.

Not quite.

The creditor writing off the debt does not mean the borrower no longer owes it.

The FTC specifically warns that after a creditor charges off a debt, the consumer still owes it. The creditor may sell the debt to a debt collector, which may then attempt to collect it. FTC: Debt and Charge-Offs

Apparently:

“We wrote it off.”

and

“You don't owe it anymore.”

are two entirely different sentences.

Finance does enjoy its vocabulary.

But Doesn't Your Credit Recover After Five Years?

There is no general federal rule saying that a credit score resets after five years.

Most negative credit information can generally remain on a credit report for seven years. Bankruptcies can remain longer. CFPB: How Long Information Stays on a Credit Report

But this may explain where some of the folklore originates:

The effect of negative information isn't necessarily identical throughout that entire period.

FICO says the recency, severity and frequency of missed payments matter to its scoring, and that older credit problems generally count less than more recent ones. A person who resumes paying accounts as agreed may therefore see a score improve before an old negative item finally disappears from the report. FICO: Payment History

So there isn't a magical fifth anniversary when the financial system looks at the calendar and announces:

Well, that was ages ago. We were all younger then.

The situation is considerably more complicated.

Because there isn't just one clock.

The Three Clocks of an Old Debt

This is one of the most important distinctions in this article.

When people talk about an old unpaid debt, they often combine three separate questions:

How long can negative information appear on the credit report?

How does that information affect the credit score as it gets older?

How long can someone use the courts to collect the debt?

Those are not the same question.

And the clocks don't necessarily have the courtesy to tell the same time.

Clock One: Credit Reporting

Most negative information can generally remain on a credit report for seven years. CFPB: Credit Reporting Periods

Clock Two: Credit Scoring

There isn't a universal countdown that automatically adds a specified number of points every year. A score reflects the information in the credit report when it is calculated, and the effect of a particular event depends on the person's overall credit profile. FICO says older negative events generally have less impact than recent ones. FICO: Payment History

Clock Three: The Statute of Limitations

Now we arrive at the clock that can cause real trouble.

A statute of limitations can restrict how long a creditor or debt collector has to sue over an unpaid debt.

The CFPB says most state or jurisdictional limitation periods for debts fall somewhere between three and six years, although some are longer. The applicable period can depend upon the type of debt, the state and even the state law specified in the credit agreement. CFPB: Old Debts and Statutes of Limitations

And then comes the part everybody should know.

Be Careful With the “Bad Person Clock”

Suppose a debt is old.

Very old.

Ancient enough that you've forgotten what you bought with the credit card in the first place.

Then one afternoon somebody calls.

They mention a creditor you vaguely remember.

They mention an amount.

And being a perfectly civilized person, you say:

“Oh, yes. I remember that. That's mine.”

Careful.

In some states, making a partial payment or acknowledging that an old debt is yours may restart the statute-of-limitations period. The CFPB warns that this may occur even after the previous limitations period has expired. CFPB: Can Collectors Collect an Old Debt?

The FTC gives an especially useful distinction: in some states, making a payment or acknowledging in writing that you owe a time-barred debt can reset the clock and begin a new limitations period. FTC: Debt Collection FAQs

Yes.

You may have just restarted a legal clock by trying to be agreeable.

Finance really does punish small talk.

But this requires an important warning.

Restarting the statute-of-limitations clock is not the same thing as restarting the credit-reporting clock.

They are governed by different rules.

Acknowledging an old debt does not simply make accurate seven-year-old negative information young again for credit-reporting purposes.

This is exactly why the clocks must not be confused.

Never Confuse Acknowledging a Claim With Verifying a Claim

If a collector contacts you about an old debt, the first objective isn't to be agreeable.

And it isn't to be disagreeable.

It's to determine what the claim actually is.

Who is the collector?

Who was the original creditor?

What amount is supposedly owed?

Does the debt belong to you?

When did the delinquency occur?

When was the last payment?

What documentation supports the claim?

What state's law applies?

Has the applicable statute of limitations expired?

The CFPB cautions that determining the correct limitations period can be complicated because contracts, payments and changes of residence can affect which rules apply. CFPB: Statutes of Limitations on Debt

So before casually agreeing that an old debt belongs to you—or sending five dollars because somebody says it will demonstrate “good faith”—find out what you're dealing with.

If you genuinely don't recognize the debt or believe the amount is wrong, federal debt-collection rules also provide mechanisms for disputing it and requesting verification. CFPB: Disputing a Debt

Never confuse acknowledging a claim with verifying a claim.

Those are different activities.

One involves evidence.

The other may have legal consequences.

What If the Statute of Limitations Has Already Expired?

Another surprise:

The debt does not necessarily disappear.

The CFPB says debt collectors in most states may continue attempting to collect a debt after the statute of limitations expires, provided their collection practices comply with applicable law.

But a debt collector covered by federal law cannot sue—or threaten to sue— to collect a time-barred debt. CFPB: Time-Barred Debt

There is another wrinkle worth knowing.

If someone is sued over a debt that is already time-barred, the CFPB warns that the consumer may still need to appear in court and raise expiration of the statute of limitations as a defense. Ignoring the lawsuit can potentially result in a judgment despite the age of the debt. CFPB: Old Debt Lawsuits

So:

Old does not necessarily mean gone.

Charged off does not necessarily mean forgiven.

No longer appearing on a credit report does not necessarily mean nonexistent.

Time-barred does not necessarily mean nobody will ever ask you to pay it.

And credit reporting is not the same thing as debt collection.

The vocabulary matters.

The Debt Didn't Disappear. It Changed Categories.

This may be the easiest way to understand the whole business.

A credit-card account begins as an ordinary account.

Payments stop.

It becomes delinquent.

Eventually the creditor may charge it off.

The debt may be assigned or sold for collection.

Years may pass.

Its treatment on a credit report may change.

The statute of limitations may eventually expire.

But those events describe different legal, accounting and credit-reporting consequences.

One event does not automatically cause all the others.

That's why the sentence:

“That debt is too old to matter.”

isn't particularly useful.

Too old for what?

Too old to appear on a typical credit report?

Too old to substantially influence a particular credit score?

Too old for a collector to sue?

Those are three different research questions.

Now we're getting somewhere.

Your Credit Score Is Not Your Financial Worth

Credit scores have acquired a strange cultural status.

People celebrate an 800 as though they have been knighted.

They apologize for a 620 as though it indicates a regrettable moral lapse.

Perhaps we have become a little too emotionally attached to somebody else's risk-management tool.

A strong credit score can certainly be financially useful.

A weak one can make borrowing more difficult or expensive.

Those consequences are real.

Your credit score measures neither your worth nor your wealth.

A person can have an excellent credit score and almost no emergency savings.

Someone else can have substantial savings and relatively little credit history.

And someone can improve a credit score while simultaneously making terrible financial decisions elsewhere.

The score has a job.

Let it do its job.

There is no need to promote it to Supreme Judge of Human Achievement.

The Strange Incentives of Credit

Consumers are frequently told that establishing credit can help them qualify for future credit.

So they obtain credit partly to establish a history demonstrating that they can responsibly handle credit.

The system then evaluates how responsibly they handled the credit they obtained partly because they wanted the system to know they could responsibly handle credit.

Finance occasionally develops a sense of humor.

None of this means consumers should borrow money unnecessarily merely to chase a number.

A credit score is one financial tool.

So is savings.

So is manageable debt.

So is adequate insurance.

So is an emergency fund.

And so is the ability to look at a loan offer and say:

No, thank you.

Follow the Data

Our previous research taught us to follow the money.

Credit reporting requires another trail.

Follow the data.

Who created the information?

Who reported it?

Who collected it?

Who transformed it into a score?

Who accessed it?

What decision was made with it?

Can you see it?

Can you correct it?

Who is responsible if it's wrong?

These questions are much more useful than obsessing over whether your score went up four points on Tuesday.

Then Follow the Money

Now ask the second set of questions.

Who pays for credit information?

Who pays for scoring products?

Who benefits from increasingly accurate predictions of borrower risk?

What does a lender gain from distinguishing one risk category from another?

What does the consumer gain?

What does the consumer pay?

Where do those interests align?

And where don't they?

This is where financial literacy becomes more interesting than memorizing that 850 is a very nice number.

We're no longer merely examining the number.

We're examining the system around the number.

For Young Researchers: Know the Players

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)

The CFPB provides consumer guidance about credit reports, credit scores, consumer-reporting companies, disputes and debt collection. Its materials are particularly useful when researching consumer rights and the distinction between credit reporting and debt collection.

Visit CFPB Credit Reports & Scores

Federal Trade Commission (FTC)

The FTC enforces federal consumer-protection laws within its jurisdiction and publishes guidance about credit, debt collection and old debts.

Visit FTC Debt Collection FAQs

Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)

The Fair Credit Reporting Act is a principal federal law governing consumer-reporting information, including matters involving permissible purposes, accuracy and consumer rights.

FTC: Fair Credit Reporting Act

Equifax, Experian and TransUnion

These are the three nationwide credit-reporting companies. They are private businesses, not federal agencies.

FICO

FICO is a private analytics company that develops credit-scoring models. When we use FICO's materials to describe its scoring system, remember that we are using a company source describing its own product.

FICO: What's in Your Credit Score?

That source distinction is deliberate.

A regulator describing an industry and a company describing its own product can both provide useful information.

They aren't the same kind of source.

Try the Research Yourself

Obtain your reports through AnnualCreditReport.com.

Then don't begin with your score.

Begin with the information.

Do you recognize every account?

Are the balances accurate?

Are payments being reported correctly?

Are there accounts you never opened?

Are old negative items being reported within the applicable time limits?

Do all three nationwide reports contain the same accounts?

If something looks wrong, don't begin by asking:

“How do I raise my score?”

Ask:

Why is this information here?

That's a much better research question.

Researcher's Note: Watch the Language

Credit reporting is an excellent demonstration of how one careless word can distort financial research.

Credit report is not the same as credit score.

FICO Score is not synonymous with every credit score.

Charge-off does not mean debt forgiveness. FTC

Time-barred does not necessarily mean the debt no longer exists. CFPB

Statute of limitations is not the same as the credit-reporting period.

And acknowledging a debt is not the same thing as verifying that a collector's claim is accurate.

Words matter.

Especially when money—and lawyers—are attached to them.

Bottom Line

A credit score can matter.

So learn how it works.

But don't worship it.

Know who created the information behind it.

Know who reported it.

Know who transformed it into a score.

Check the file.

Correct errors.

And when an old debt suddenly crawls out of a filing cabinet after years of silence, don't assume that charged off, seven years old, time-barred, and no longer owed all mean the same thing.

They don't.

Ask who is making the claim.

Ask for the documentation.

Find out which clock is actually running.

And before casually acknowledging an old debt or making a token payment, understand what that action could mean under the law that applies to you.

Never confuse acknowledging a claim with verifying a claim.

Then follow the data.

Follow the money.

And check the receipts.

No political team required. Just the receipts. 🧾
