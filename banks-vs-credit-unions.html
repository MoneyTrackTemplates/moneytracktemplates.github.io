Banks vs. Credit Unions: What's Actually Different?

Ownership, voting power, insurance, fees, membership, regulation—and where your money really goes.

Research & drafting: OpenAI's ChatGPT
Editing & publication: MoneyTrackTemplates
Research reviewed: September 26, 2026

Walk into a bank and a credit union and the experience may look remarkably similar.

Both may offer checking accounts, savings accounts, debit cards, automobile loans, mortgages, mobile apps, automated teller machines, online banking, and physical branches.

So why do we have two different kinds of institutions?

The biggest difference isn't the debit card.

It is ownership.

A commercial bank and a credit union can perform many of the same financial functions while operating under fundamentally different organizational structures.

And those structures affect some very important questions:

  • Who owns the institution?
  • Who gets to vote?
  • Where do earnings go?
  • Who regulates it?
  • Who insures your deposits or shares?
  • Who can become a customer or member?
  • Can another corporation own it?
  • Can a foreign company control it?
  • Who is accountable for what happens to your money?
Let's follow the trail.

🏦 What Is a Bank?

A commercial bank is a financial institution that accepts deposits, makes loans, and provides other financial services.

Banks can have shareholders.

A bank may also be controlled by another corporation known as a bank holding company.

The Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council (FFIEC) describes a bank holding company as a company that controls one or more United States banks.

A bank holding company can itself be owned by another bank holding company, creating an ownership chain. The organization at the top of the reported hierarchy may be identified as the top holder.

Depositor → Bank → Bank Holding Company → Parent Company → Top Holder

As we learned in Banking Basics, that ownership trail can sometimes extend outside the United States.

Explore FFIEC Institution Types →

🤝 What Is a Credit Union?

A credit union uses a very different organizational structure.

The National Credit Union Administration (NCUA) describes credit unions as not-for-profit, member-owned, and member-controlled cooperative financial institutions.

Members don't merely use the credit union.

They collectively own it.

A federal credit union is governed by a board of directors elected from its membership.

And here is one of the most interesting differences:

Each member generally has one vote regardless of the number of shares the member owns.

Someone with a much larger account balance doesn't receive proportionally more voting power simply because more money is deposited.

That is fundamentally different from conventional corporate shareholder voting.

🔎 Follow the Ownership

Commercial Bank

A bank can have a corporate ownership chain.

Bank → Immediate Parent → Holding Company → Top Holder → Owners

Public securities filings may provide additional information about significant disclosed beneficial owners where applicable.

Credit Union

A credit union operates as a member-owned cooperative.

Member → Credit Union → Member-Elected Board

The governance trail therefore differs from conventional shareholder ownership.

That doesn't mean credit unions are free from management problems, regulatory problems, conflicts, poor decisions, or financial failure.

It means the ownership structure is different.

The scale and reporting populations of the banking and credit-union sectors differ.

For the first quarter of 2026, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) reported results from 4,278 insured commercial banks and savings institutions. Those institutions reported approximately $80.5 billion in aggregate quarterly net income.

👥 Who Really Owns a Credit Union?

The phrase member-owned deserves more explanation.

When someone joins a federally chartered credit union, deposits are generally called shares.

Those shares help fund the credit union's lending, investments, and other operations.

Membership gives eligible members governance rights, including the right to vote for credit-union officials and, subject to applicable requirements, to seek election as an official.

The National Credit Union Administration explains that federal credit unions are governed by boards elected by and from the membership.

"Member-owned" isn't merely an advertising slogan.

It describes a legal and organizational structure.

It doesn't mean each member personally owns a particular percentage of the building, computers, loan portfolio, or cash.

It means the institution operates as a cooperative owned and controlled collectively by its members.

🗳️ One Member, One Vote

This deserves its own section because it is one of the clearest differences between the two systems.

Federal credit-union members exercise democratic control.

One member. One vote.

The amount a member has deposited doesn't increase the member's voting power.

Compare that with a publicly traded corporate structure, where voting power is commonly associated with the number and class of voting shares held.

That doesn't automatically make one governance system superior to the other.

It means they answer the question "Who controls this organization?" differently.

🌎 Can a Credit Union Be Foreign-Owned?

A conventional commercial bank operating in the United States can be controlled by a foreign banking organization.

A federal credit union does not operate through that conventional outside-shareholder ownership structure.

The National Credit Union Administration describes federal credit unions as member-owned and member-controlled cooperatives rather than institutions owned through conventional outside holding-company or investor structures.

So when investigating international connections involving a credit union, we should ask somewhat different questions.

  • What is the membership structure?
  • Who sits on the board?
  • Who are the senior managers where relevant?
  • What material service-provider relationships exist?
  • What investment relationships are relevant?
  • What correspondent or corporate credit-union relationships exist?
  • What financial-technology providers are involved?
  • What do official regulatory records show?
A foreign business relationship isn't the same thing as foreign ownership.

Name the relationship correctly.

🚪 Why Can't Everyone Join Every Credit Union?

Banks generally offer accounts to customers who satisfy their account-opening requirements.

Credit unions introduce another concept:

Field of membership.

Credit unions are legally limited to serving people within the fields of membership established by their charters.

Depending upon the credit union, eligibility may be based on occupation, association, community, geographic area, or another qualifying group.

Modern credit-union membership can sometimes be much broader than consumers expect.

But eligibility still matters.

Before comparing a particular credit union with a bank, ask:

Can I actually become a member?

💰 Where Do the Earnings Go?

This is another area where terminology matters.

Commercial banks can generate profits for their owners or shareholders.

Credit unions are organized as not-for-profit cooperatives serving their members rather than conventional outside shareholders.

That structure can allow credit unions to return economic value to members through combinations of rates, fees, services, reserves, and other member benefits.

But don't turn an organizational structure into a promise about a particular account.

A credit union can charge fees.

A bank can offer a no-fee account.

A bank can offer an excellent savings rate.

A credit union can offer an unimpressive one.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) confirms that banks and credit unions can charge monthly maintenance or service fees and advises consumers to compare account requirements and fee schedules.

We won't write:

"Credit unions always have lower fees."

We'll write:

"Compare the actual fee schedule."

🛡️ FDIC vs. NCUSIF: Who Insures Your Money?

Here are two acronyms worth learning.

Banks

Eligible deposits at insured banks are generally protected by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC).

Credit Unions

Eligible shares at federally insured credit unions are generally protected by the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund (NCUSIF), administered by the National Credit Union Administration (NCUA).

The National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund was established by Congress in 1970 and is backed by the full faith and credit of the United States.

For common individual-account arrangements, both federal systems generally provide insurance protection up to at least $250,000, subject to the applicable ownership-category rules and other requirements.

Bank? Look for FDIC.

Credit union? Look for NCUA/NCUSIF federal share insurance.

But don't stop at the logo.

Verify the institution.

🔎 Insurance Is About the Institution, Not the Sign Outside

Consumers should independently verify insurance.

For banks, use the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation's BankFind Suite.

For credit unions, use the National Credit Union Administration's Credit Union Locator and share-insurance resources.

And don't assume every financial product sold inside a bank or credit union is insured.

Stocks, bonds, mutual funds, annuities, and other investment products do not become federally insured deposits or shares simply because they are sold through an insured financial institution.

The building doesn't insure the product.

The rules do.

🧭 Follow the Money: Bank vs. Credit Union

Commercial Bank

You deposit money.

The bank owes that deposit to you.

The bank uses its assets and funding to make loans, hold securities, and conduct other banking activities.

Depending upon its organization, profits can ultimately benefit corporate owners or shareholders.

Depositor → Bank → Parent → Top Holder → Owners

Credit Union

You become a member and deposit funds as shares.

Member shares provide important funding for loans, investments, and other operations.

Members collectively own the cooperative and elect its board.

Member → Credit Union → Member-Elected Board

Same basic consumer activity—depositing money.

Very different governance trail.

🧾 Fees: Never Assume

A financial institution's organizational type doesn't tell you the entire price of doing business there.

Before opening an account, examine:

  • Monthly maintenance fees
  • Minimum-balance requirements
  • Overdraft fees and policies
  • Insufficient-funds policies
  • Out-of-network automated teller machine fees
  • Wire-transfer fees
  • Paper-statement fees
  • Check fees
  • Foreign-transaction fees
  • Early-account-closing fees
  • Dormant-account fees
  • Requirements for waiving monthly fees

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that banks and credit unions may charge account-maintenance fees and that consumers should review the institution's disclosures and fee schedule.

Read the fee schedule.

It's much more informative than the commercial.

📱 Technology: Don't Assume Here Either

Another old stereotype deserves retirement.

A bank isn't automatically technologically sophisticated because it's a bank.

A credit union isn't automatically technologically outdated because it's a credit union.

Compare the actual institution.

  • Mobile banking
  • Mobile check deposit
  • Bill payment
  • Person-to-person transfers
  • Card controls
  • Fraud alerts
  • Automated teller machine network
  • Online statements
  • Customer-service availability
  • Accessibility
  • Integration with financial apps

And remember what we learned in Banking Basics: a bank or credit union can rely on outside financial-technology providers.

The pretty app on your phone doesn't necessarily tell you who built or operates every system behind it.

📊 Americans Are Already Banking by Phone

This isn't a minor change in consumer behavior.

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation's 2023 National Survey of Unbanked and Underbanked Households found that 48.3 percent of banked households used mobile banking as their primary method of account access.

The survey also found that 4.2 percent of U.S. households, approximately 5.6 million households, were unbanked in 2023, meaning no one in the household had a checking or savings account at a bank or credit union.

The modern competition isn't simply:

Bank vs. credit union.

Increasingly it is:

Bank vs. credit union vs. financial technology platform vs. nonbank payment service—or some combination of them.

🏛️ Who Regulates Credit Unions?

Just as not every bank has exactly the same regulator, not every credit union has exactly the same regulatory arrangement.

Credit unions can be:

  • Federally chartered
  • State chartered

The National Credit Union Administration (NCUA) charters and supervises federal credit unions.

It also administers the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund, which insures all federal credit unions and most state-chartered credit unions.

State-chartered credit unions also operate under applicable state supervisory systems.

What exact institution am I examining, what is its charter, and who supervises it?

⚖️ Member-Owned Does Not Mean Problem-Free

The cooperative structure of a credit union doesn't make misconduct, poor management, excessive fees, financial problems, or conflicts impossible.

Credit unions can fail.

Credit unions can receive regulatory orders.

Credit unions can make bad loans.

Credit unions can have governance disputes.

Credit unions can charge fees.

Member-owned is a description of governance—not a certificate of perfection.

Exactly the same evidence rule we use for banks applies here: check the records.

📜 Policy & Legislative Trail

The American credit-union system has its own history.

The National Credit Union Administration's historical material identifies early advocates for cooperative credit, including Edward Filene and Roy Bergengren.

The federal system took a major step on June 26, 1934, when President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Federal Credit Union Act into law.

The newly created Federal Credit Union Division was initially placed within the Farm Credit Administration.

Later in 1934, the Morris Sheppard Federal Credit Union in Texarkana, Texas, became the first federally chartered credit union.

Federal share insurance came later.

Congress established the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund (NCUSIF) in 1970.

Cooperative-credit movement → Federal Credit Union Act → Federal chartering system → Federal share insurance → Modern National Credit Union Administration

✅ Before You Choose a Bank or Credit Union

1. Verify the institution.

Is the bank actually insured by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation? Is the credit union federally insured through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund?

2. Identify the ownership structure.

If it's a bank, investigate its parent and top holder. If it's a credit union, investigate its charter, membership structure, and board.

3. Read the fee schedule.

Don't rely on "free checking" without reading the conditions.

4. Compare actual rates.

Compare savings rates, loan rates, and the applicable Annual Percentage Yield (APY) or Annual Percentage Rate (APR).

Annual Percentage Yield describes the rate of return on a deposit account taking compounding into account.

Annual Percentage Rate is commonly used to express the annualized cost of borrowing under applicable disclosure rules.

5. Check access.

Branches? Automated teller machines? Mobile app? Telephone support? Weekend service?

6. Check membership eligibility.

For a credit union, determine whether you qualify to join.

7. Investigate regulatory history.

Look beyond the institution's own website.

8. Ask where your money actually goes.

Especially when a financial app or third-party service sits between you and the institution.
