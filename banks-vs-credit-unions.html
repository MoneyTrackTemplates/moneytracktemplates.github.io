Banks vs. Credit Unions:
What's Actually Different?

They may both offer checking accounts, savings accounts, cards and loans — but banks and credit unions are not organized in quite the same way. Here's what that means for the person opening the account.

Research & drafting: OpenAI's ChatGPT
Editing & publication: MoneyTrackTemplates
Research reviewed: September 24, 2026

Walk into a bank and a credit union and the experience can look remarkably similar. Both may offer checking and savings accounts, debit cards, online banking, certificates and loans.

The important differences are underneath the counter: who owns the institution, who can use it, how it is chartered and regulated, and which federal insurance system may protect qualifying deposits or shares.

Plain English: You do not need to decide that banks are "good" and credit unions are "better," or the other way around. They are different organizational models. The useful question is whether a particular institution and account fit what you need.

1. Start With the Biggest Difference: Ownership

🏦 Banks

Many banks are for-profit businesses. Depending on their corporate structure, ownership may ultimately belong to shareholders or other owners of the banking organization.

Banks can range from small community institutions to very large organizations operating across many states.

🤝 Credit Unions

Credit unions are cooperative financial institutions owned by their members. A person generally becomes a member by meeting the credit union's eligibility requirements and opening the required membership share account.

Membership requirements are often described as a credit union's field of membership.

2. A Quick Side-by-Side Comparison

Question Bank Credit Union
Basic structure Typically a for-profit banking institution. Member-owned cooperative.
Customers Generally serves customers who meet account requirements. Serves members who qualify under its field of membership.
Federal insurance Qualifying deposits at an FDIC-insured bank are covered by FDIC deposit insurance. Qualifying shares at a federally insured credit union are covered by the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund.
Federal regulator Depends on charter and other factors. Federal bank regulators include the OCC, Federal Reserve and FDIC. NCUA charters and supervises federal credit unions. State-chartered credit unions can also have state supervision.
Products May include checking, savings, CDs, cards, loans and other financial services. May offer many comparable services, sometimes using terms such as share accounts and share certificates.
Rates and fees Compare the actual institution and account. Do not assume that one category will always have the lowest fees or highest rates.

3. What Does "Member-Owned" Mean?

Credit unions are organized as cooperative institutions. Instead of becoming merely a customer, an eligible person becomes a member-owner of the credit union.

This is also why credit-union terminology can look slightly different. What consumers commonly call a savings account may be called a share account. A certificate of deposit may be described as a share certificate.

Translation: Don't let the vocabulary intimidate you. When comparing accounts, concentrate on what the product actually does, its interest or dividend rate, fees, withdrawal rules, insurance status and other account terms.

4. FDIC vs. NCUA: Two Different Insurance Systems

This is one of the most important distinctions to understand.

🛡️ FDIC

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation insures qualifying deposits at FDIC-insured banks.

The standard insurance amount is $250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, for each account ownership category.

Verify at FDIC →

🛡️ NCUA / NCUSIF

The National Credit Union Administration administers the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund, which insures qualifying accounts at federally insured credit unions.

Coverage depends on ownership category and applicable NCUA rules, so members should verify their particular accounts rather than relying on a general assumption.

Verify at NCUA →

🔍 The Logo Is Not Enough — Verify

Before depositing substantial savings, verify the institution using the federal regulator's official tools.

Also verify the product. An insured institution may offer financial products that are not themselves insured deposits or insured shares.

5. Does One Always Pay Better Rates?

No rule says a credit union will always pay a higher savings rate, charge a lower loan rate or have fewer fees than every bank.

Likewise, a bank's size or familiar name does not tell you whether a particular account is competitive.

Rates, fees, minimum balances, ATM access, branch availability, digital tools and account requirements can vary from institution to institution and can change over time.

MoneyTrack rule: Compare the account, not merely the logo on the building.

6. What Is a Credit Union's "Field of Membership"?

A credit union generally has rules describing who is eligible to join. Eligibility can be connected to employment, an organization, family relationships, military affiliation, geography or another qualifying group permitted under applicable law and the credit union's charter.

Modern fields of membership vary considerably, so a person who assumes that a credit union is unavailable may discover that they actually qualify.

The safest approach is simply to check the membership requirements of the specific credit union.

Practical Money Check

✅ Before You Open an Account

Whether you choose a bank or credit union, check these items before moving your money.

☐ 1. Verify the institution.
Search FDIC BankFind for a bank or the NCUA's official tools for a federally insured credit union.
☐ 2. Identify the exact account.
Checking? Savings? Money market deposit account? Certificate? Investment product? The label matters.
☐ 3. Check the insurance.
Determine which federal insurance system applies and whether the product itself qualifies.
☐ 4. Read the fee schedule.
Look for monthly maintenance fees, overdraft fees, ATM fees, wire fees and other charges that could apply.
☐ 5. Check minimum requirements.
Find out whether the account requires a minimum opening deposit, minimum balance or particular activity.
☐ 6. Compare APY — not just advertising.
When comparing savings products, look at the annual percentage yield and any conditions required to receive an advertised rate.
☐ 7. Check access.
Consider branches, ATMs, mobile banking, customer service, transfers and how easily you can reach your money when you need it.
☐ 8. Keep the account disclosures.
Save a copy of the terms, fee schedule and disclosures that apply when you open the account.

Follow the Law

🏛️ Policy & Legislative Trail

Credit unions did not simply appear as an alternative to banks. Their development involved a broader cooperative movement, state legislation and eventually federal legislation.

1900s–1920s — The Credit Union Movement Expands Credit-union advocates worked to establish cooperative financial institutions and state laws authorizing them.
1921 — Credit Union National Extension Bureau Edward Filene and Roy Bergengren organized the Credit Union National Extension Bureau to promote credit unions and credit-union legislation.
1932 — A Federal Law Is Discussed According to the NCUA's historical timeline, Roy Bergengren met with U.S. Senator Morris Sheppard of Texas to discuss the need for federal credit-union legislation.
1934 — Federal Credit Union Act President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Federal Credit Union Act on June 26, 1934. The law established a federal system for chartering credit unions.
1970 — Federal Share Insurance Federal legislation established the National Credit Union Administration and created the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund, adding a federal insurance system for qualifying credit-union member accounts.

7. Who Helped Shape the Federal Credit Union System?

MoneyTrackTemplates does not assume that a law was the work of one person merely because one person's name is associated with it. We identify documented roles whenever the historical record supports them.

The NCUA's historical account identifies Massachusetts businessman Edward Filene as an important early supporter of the American credit-union movement and credits Roy Bergengren with helping develop the modern credit-union system.

The same NCUA history records Bergengren's 1932 meeting with Senator Morris Sheppard of Texas concerning federal credit-union legislation.

President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Federal Credit Union Act into law on June 26, 1934.

Why we describe roles this way: Sponsoring a bill, advocating for an idea, drafting provisions, serving on a committee, voting on legislation and signing a bill into law are different historical actions. Where the evidence permits, MoneyTrackTemplates will tell you which role the historical record documents rather than collapsing all of them into the word "created."

8. So Which One Should You Choose?

That depends on what you need from the particular account.

Instead of choosing solely because an institution is called a bank or a credit union, compare the things that affect you: account terms, fees, APY, loan rates, eligibility, branch and ATM access, digital services, customer support and federal insurance.

Two banks can be very different from each other. Two credit unions can also be very different from each other.

The useful question isn't: "Are banks or credit unions better?"

It is: "What does this particular institution offer, what does it cost, how is my money protected, and does it fit what I need?"
