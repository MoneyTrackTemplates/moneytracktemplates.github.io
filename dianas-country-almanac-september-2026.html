Welcome To September

The Harvest Begins

September arrives with one hand full of tomatoes and the other reaching for a sweater. Summer has not surrendered, yet autumn is already moving into the evenings. The light turns mellow, the birds grow restless and every cool morning feels like a private announcement.

This is the month of useful abundance. Gardens offer their last warm-weather treasures while orchards begin filling baskets with apples and pears. The pantry asks to be counted, the freezer asks to be organized and the household begins making quiet bargains with winter.

In this issue, we gather food, stories and sensible preparations. We visit the September garden, watch animals respond to the changing season, study the Harvest Moon, open an old book of poetry and investigate a celebration whose secrets have remained safe for more than two thousand years.

Meet Frida

Frida is our Balinese savings cat and the cheerful keeper of the household ledger. She was inspired by a much-loved real cat, remembered here for her companionship and a little mischief. Across these pages she will inspect the pantry, supervise the budget and occasionally recommend catnip as an essential expense.

Welcome to September—the month that keeps summer’s sweetness and adds autumn’s good sense.

How to Use This Almanac

Read it from beginning to end, or wander according to appetite. Gardeners may turn first to the soil, cooks to the soup pot, stargazers to the Moon and the merely curious to the mysteries. Dates are given for 2026; gardening and weather advice must always be adjusted to local conditions.

September Is…

September is National Preparedness Month, a useful invitation to check emergency supplies, household plans and pet arrangements before trouble chooses the least convenient afternoon. It is also Library Card Sign-Up Month and National Food Safety Education Month—three observances that improve a household without requiring decorative pillows.

  • National Preparedness Month: make a plan, build or refresh a kit and include pets.
  • Library Card Sign-Up Month: borrow before buying when a book, class or tool will do.
  • Food Safety Education Month: mind clean hands, safe temperatures and prompt refrigeration.
  • National Honey Month: appreciate the bees, then keep the jar lid clean.
Faith, Feast Days & The Turning Year

September Bells

The Christian calendar gives September several bright markers. The Nativity of the Blessed Virgin Mary is observed on September 8, the Exaltation of the Holy Cross on September 14 and the archangels Michael, Gabriel and Raphael on September 29. These days carried faith into the household through churchgoing, shared meals, seasonal work and the names given to fairs and market days.

Michaelmas

Michaelmas stood near harvest’s end in much of Britain and Ireland. Rents were settled, workers hired, school terms begun and geese placed in grave danger of becoming dinner. The feast acquired weather sayings and local customs because a major day on the calendar naturally collected whatever the neighborhood happened to be doing.

A household need not roast a goose to appreciate the old rhythm. Michaelmas may simply serve as a late-September reminder to settle an account, finish an outdoor task and enter the colder season with fewer loose ends.

Before the Church Bells

Long before Christian feast days shaped Europe’s autumn calendar, farming communities marked harvest by offering food, holding communal meals and thanking the powers they believed governed field and weather. The evidence varies sharply by place and century; there was no single ancient ‘European harvest ritual,’ however convenient that would be for modern calendars.

What survives is a patchwork: archaeology, Roman descriptions, inscriptions, later chronicles and customs recorded after conversion. Some practices are well documented; others arrive wearing several centuries of embroidery. We will label them accordingly and leave the invented ceremonies to people with more candles than evidence.

Historical Mystery

What Happened at Eleusis?

Each year in ancient Greece, initiates traveled to Eleusis for secret rites connected with Demeter, goddess of grain, and Persephone, whose descent and return mirrored loss, winter and renewed growth. The Greater Mysteries were held around the month corresponding roughly to September or October, though ancient calendars do not fit neatly inside ours.

Participants prepared, processed from Athens, fasted, drank a barley beverage called kykeon and entered the sanctuary. Then the record goes politely dark. Initiates were forbidden to reveal the central rites, and most kept the promise with irritating success.

Ancient writers hint that sacred objects were shown, words were spoken and a dramatic experience altered the initiate’s view of death. Modern scholars have proposed ceremonies, visions, performances and substances, but no theory has emptied the locked drawer.

What we know: the Mysteries endured for centuries and drew people from across the Greek world. What remains uncertain: precisely what initiates saw and did. A secret kept by thousands for generations is either a triumph of devotion or history’s most successful meeting policy.

Evidence label: The festival and its secrecy are well documented. Reconstructions of the hidden rites remain disputed.

This Month In History

September Leaves Its Mark

September 2, 1666 — London Burns

The Great Fire of London began in a bakery on Pudding Lane and burned for several days, destroying much of the old city. Its spread revealed the danger of crowded timber buildings, narrow lanes and dry weather. The rebuilding changed London’s streets and building practices, though not as quickly or neatly as later storytellers sometimes suggest.

September 3, 1783 — Peace Is Signed

The Treaty of Paris formally ended the American Revolutionary War and recognized the independence of the United States. A new nation had won its place on paper; the considerably harder business of governing it remained.

September 23, 1846 — Neptune Is Found

Astronomers discovered Neptune after mathematics predicted where the unseen planet should be. Johann Galle and Heinrich d’Arrest observed it near the position calculated from work associated with Urbain Le Verrier. It was a splendid moment for arithmetic, which rarely receives such dramatic lighting.

September 28, 1928 — A Useful Mold

Alexander Fleming noticed that mold had inhibited bacteria in a laboratory dish, an observation that helped begin the story of penicillin. Developing a practical drug required later work by many researchers. History often remembers the surprised observer and misplaces the team carrying the heavy furniture.

September 5, 1977 — Voyager 1 Departs

NASA launched Voyager 1 toward the outer planets. After visiting Jupiter and Saturn, the spacecraft continued outward and entered interstellar space. It carries a golden record of sounds and images from Earth—proof that humanity can prepare a thoughtful introduction when given enough notice.

From The Old Almanac Shelf

Then, Now & Next

Then: The Harvest Was the Calendar

For much of history, September’s chief appointment was whatever ripened next. Families and neighbors cut grain, gathered fruit, dried herbs and stored roots while daylight and weather allowed. The clock mattered less than the field, which has never accepted a request to reschedule.

Now: The Household Harvest

Most of us no longer bring in acres of grain, but the old principle still travels well: gather what is useful, preserve what will keep and do not waste the season’s plenty. A freezer inventory, repaired coat and paid bill are modest harvests, yet winter recognizes them.

Next: Leave Something Better

Plant a tree, save seed from a suitable open-pollinated plant, teach a recipe, record a family story or begin a small emergency fund. Not every September task must serve the present household alone.

September Maxims

  • A full basket is pleasant; a labeled freezer is better.
  • The last tomato deserves respect, not another week on the windowsill.
  • Prepare for weather while the sky is still behaving.
  • He who owns three umbrellas will meet the rain while carrying none.
  • Frida advises saving first and browsing catnip catalogs second.
The September Garden

One Month, Many Climates

September is not one gardening season. In northern gardens, frost may already be making threats; in temperate regions, summer crops and fall seedlings share the beds; across warm-winter areas, the chief cool-season garden may just be beginning. Local extension guidance, frost dates, elevation and rainfall outrank any national calendar.

RegionSeptember Work
Cool northern & high-elevationHarvest tender crops, watch frost forecasts, sow quick greens where time permits and protect what can continue.
Temperate middle regionsKeep productive summer crops, begin spinach, lettuce, radish and other cool-season plantings as temperatures allow.
Southern & warm-winterPrepare beds and begin the main fall garden according to local heat and planting dates.
Hot desert & tropicalFollow rainfall, heat and local seasons rather than the word ‘autumn’; shade and water management may still rule.

What to Do Now

  • Harvest ripe vegetables often; an overgrown zucchini is a vegetable’s attempt to become furniture.
  • Remove diseased plants and discard them as local guidance recommends.
  • Leave healthy seed heads for birds where practical.
  • Add finished compost to empty beds; avoid piling mulch against stems or trunks.
  • Adjust irrigation to actual weather and soil moisture.
  • Clean tools before storage and sharpen what has spent summer pretending to cut.
  • Photograph the garden and note what succeeded, failed or required diplomatic intervention.

Saving Seed

Choose healthy, mature seed from suitable open-pollinated plants, dry it thoroughly and label the envelope with variety and year. Hybrid plants may not produce offspring like the parent, and some crops cross readily with relatives. A mystery seed is charming only until it occupies half the garden.

The Last Tomato

Do not uproot a healthy plant merely because a calendar has changed. Let productive crops continue until weather, disease or declining quality gives the order. Before frost, mature green tomatoes may ripen indoors; damaged or spoiled fruit belongs elsewhere than the pantry.

Harvest Basket

What September Brings to the Table

September joins late summer and early autumn in one generous basket. Availability varies, but many markets offer tomatoes, peppers, corn, beans, grapes, apples, pears, figs, melons, winter squash and the last peaches. Buy what looks good rather than forcing a recipe upon tired produce.

Tomatoes

Use the ripest fruit for soup, sauce or roasting. Refrigerate cut tomatoes promptly. For canning, follow a current tested recipe with the specified acidification, jar size and processing method; a family recipe may be beloved without being shelf-safe.

Apples & Pears

Choose firm fruit without bruised or broken skin. Eat crisp varieties fresh and cook softer or tart fruit into sauce, butter or dessert. Store sound fruit cool and apart from produce that is sensitive to ethylene.

Grapes

Refrigerate unwashed grapes and rinse them just before eating. Freeze loose grapes for a simple treat, though Frida considers anything that rolls across the kitchen to be a recreational object.

Winter Squash

Harvest mature squash with the stem attached and cure only varieties for which curing is recommended. Store in a cool, dry, ventilated place and inspect occasionally. One soft spot can develop ambitions.

Four Small Preserving Projects

  • Freeze chopped peppers on a tray, then transfer to labeled freezer containers.
  • Dry culinary herbs using reliable temperature and storage guidance.
  • Make refrigerator pickles for prompt cold storage—not shelf storage.
  • Cook applesauce for the refrigerator or freezer, or use a tested canning recipe.
The Country Kitchen

Four September Recipes

These recipes move from the bright garden to the warmer table without asking the cook to spend the entire month beside the stove. They are cooking recipes, not instructions for shelf-stable canning.

Farmhouse Tomato Soup

Serves 4 • About 50 minutes

Ingredients: 2 tablespoons olive oil; 1 chopped medium onion; 2 minced garlic cloves; 5 cups chopped ripe tomatoes; 2 cups low-sodium chicken or vegetable broth; 1 teaspoon salt; ½ teaspoon black pepper; 1 tablespoon chopped basil; 1 teaspoon thyme; and ½ cup heavy cream, optional.

  1. Heat the oil over medium heat. Cook the onion 5–7 minutes until soft; add garlic for 30 seconds.
  2. Add tomatoes, broth, salt and pepper. Simmer uncovered for 20 minutes; stir in basil and thyme.
  3. Blend carefully until smooth. Return to the pan, add cream if desired and adjust seasoning.

Refrigerate leftovers promptly. Do not use this recipe for shelf-stable canning.

Skillet Corn with Peppers

Serves 4 • About 25 minutes

Ingredients: 4 cups fresh or frozen corn; 1 diced bell pepper; 2 tablespoons butter or olive oil; 2 sliced green onions; ½ teaspoon smoked paprika; ¼ teaspoon salt; black pepper; and 1 tablespoon lime juice.

  1. Heat butter in a large skillet over medium-high heat. Add corn and pepper; cook 8–10 minutes, stirring occasionally, until tender and lightly browned.
  2. Add green onion, paprika, salt and pepper. Cook 1 minute, remove from heat and stir in lime juice.

Apple-Oat Crisp

Serves 6 • About 55 minutes

Ingredients: 6 cups peeled sliced apples; 2 tablespoons lemon juice; ⅓ cup granulated sugar; 1 teaspoon cinnamon; ¾ cup rolled oats; ½ cup flour; ½ cup packed brown sugar; ¼ teaspoon salt; and 6 tablespoons cold butter, cubed.

  1. Heat oven to 375°F. Toss apples with lemon juice, granulated sugar and cinnamon; place in a greased 8-inch baking dish.
  2. Combine oats, flour, brown sugar and salt. Cut in butter until crumbly; scatter over apples.
  3. Bake 35–40 minutes, until bubbling and golden. Cool at least 10 minutes before serving.

Herbed Potato & Green Bean Supper

Serves 4 • About 40 minutes

Ingredients: 1½ pounds baby potatoes, halved; 12 ounces trimmed green beans; 3 tablespoons olive oil; 2 minced garlic cloves; 1 teaspoon thyme; ½ teaspoon salt; black pepper; 1 tablespoon lemon juice; and 2 chopped hard-cooked eggs, optional.

  1. Heat oven to 425°F. Toss potatoes with 2 tablespoons oil, thyme, salt and pepper. Roast cut-side down for 20 minutes.
  2. Toss beans and garlic with remaining oil. Add to the pan and roast 12–15 minutes, until the potatoes are tender and beans are lightly browned.
  3. Finish with lemon juice and eggs if using. Refrigerate leftovers within two hours.

A September Supper

Serve tomato soup with warm bread, or pair the potato-and-bean dish with roast chicken. Finish with apple crisp. The centerpiece may be flowers, a bowl of apples or the unpaid electric bill turned face down.

The September House

A Useful Reset

September favors preparation without panic. Choose one afternoon to inspect the pantry, freezer, medicine cabinet and emergency supplies. Replace what is expired, note what is missing and resist buying six of anything merely because the label says ‘value.’

Pantry & Freezer

  • Bring older food forward and plan meals around it.
  • Label frozen food with contents and date; ‘brown thing’ is not an inventory system.
  • Discard swollen, leaking, badly rusted or deeply dented cans.
  • Keep a simple list on the freezer door and cross items off as they leave.

Preparedness Without Drama

Ready.gov recommends a household plan and supplies for several days. Begin with water, nonperishable food, medications, flashlights, batteries, a radio, first-aid items, sanitation supplies, copies of important information and necessities for children, older adults, disabilities and pets. Build gradually if the full list strains the budget.

The Fall Wardrobe

Before buying, inspect coats, boots, blankets and rain gear. Mend a seam, replace a lace and wash what has spent summer in storage. Children have an unfortunate habit of growing between seasons, while adults mostly discover that last year’s coat contains a receipt and one peppermint.

A September Cleaning List

  • Test smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms as the manufacturer directs.
  • Clean dryer vents and inspect heating-system filters.
  • Wash windows where needed and check weather stripping.
  • Store summer equipment clean and dry.
  • Review insurance contacts and photograph important household contents.
Barn, Backyard & Wildwood

Animals Know the Season Is Moving

September changes the timetable for animals as surely as it changes ours. Migration gathers momentum, winter coats begin thickening and many wild creatures feed with new concentration. Observe from a respectful distance; wildlife does not require an audience close enough for introductions.

Birds on the Move

Many songbirds, raptors and shorebirds migrate during late summer and fall. Keep windows safer with visible exterior markings or screens, reduce unnecessary nighttime lighting and offer clean water. Feeders should be cleaned regularly; natural seed heads and native plants provide food without a monthly subscription.

Monarchs & Other Pollinators

Monarch migration timing varies by region and population. Late-blooming native flowers can supply nectar, while milkweed supports caterpillars during the breeding season. Avoid treating every unfamiliar insect as a declaration of war.

Deer, Bears & Busy Appetites

As natural foods change, wildlife may visit yards, orchards and unsecured trash. Keep food waste, pet food and livestock feed inaccessible according to local wildlife guidance. Never feed bears, and give deer room; an animal that looks picturesque through glass may be less decorative beside your vegetable bed.

Cats, Dogs & Shorter Days

Check collars, identification and microchip details before storm season. Keep pets away from antifreeze, rodenticides, toxic mushrooms and unfamiliar wild animals. Evening walks may require reflective gear earlier than they did in July. Indoor cats require no reflective gear but may demand supper as though darkness were a legal argument.

Livestock & the Feed Room

Inspect fencing, shelter, water systems and stored feed before cold weather. Fodder means food grown or gathered for livestock—hay, silage, grain or other feed. Store it dry and protected from pests, and consult a veterinarian or extension specialist about species-specific nutrition. The object is to keep everyone in the homestead properly fed, not to let the goats draft the menu.

Moon & Stars

The Harvest Moon

The full Moon on September 26 is the Harvest Moon because it is the full Moon nearest the September equinox. Near this season, the Moon’s path can produce several evenings of relatively early moonrise at many Northern Hemisphere latitudes, giving farmers of earlier centuries more light after sunset. The name describes a useful pattern, not a Moon wearing overalls.

The Equinox

At the September equinox, the Sun crosses the celestial equator moving southward, beginning astronomical autumn in the Northern Hemisphere and spring in the Southern. Day and night are not exactly equal everywhere because the Sun is a disk, the atmosphere bends light and local horizons refuse to be standardized.

A September Sky Walk

On a clear evening, find the Summer Triangle—Vega, Deneb and Altair—still prominent after dusk. Later, look east for the rising autumn constellations. Planet visibility changes through the month and by location, so consult NASA’s current skywatching guidance or a trusted planetarium app before promising the household a planet at a particular hour.

Poetry By The Window

James Thomson and the Turning Season

Scottish poet James Thomson was born on September 11, 1700. His long poem The Seasons helped make weather, landscape and rural work worthy subjects of serious poetry. Thomson could spend more lines describing a cloud than most of us spend answering an email, but he watched the natural world with patience.

Crowned with the sickle and the wheaten sheaf,
While Autumn, nodding o’er the yellow plain,
Comes jovial on…

These lines from Autumn place the season in motion: harvest tools, ripe grain and a figure striding across the field. The style is eighteenth-century grand, yet the sight is familiar. The year has worked a long while to reach this moment.

The Seasons is in the public domain. Readers may explore the complete poem through the Library of Congress and Wikisource.

Country Lore—Or Likely?

September Sayings Under Inspection

‘A warm first week promises a mild winter.’

Lore. A short spell of September weather cannot reliably predict an entire winter. Enjoy the warmth, but do not dismiss the snow shovel.

‘Crickets chirp faster when it is warmer.’

Likely, with qualifications. Temperature influences the chirping rate of some cricket species, and formulas can estimate temperature from their calls. Species, age and conditions affect the result; the cricket has not been certified as a thermometer.

‘Heavy acorns mean a hard winter.’

Lore. Oaks sometimes produce unusually large crops in mast years, influenced by their biology and environmental conditions. A generous crop feeds wildlife; it does not issue a seasonal forecast.

‘Birds gather before a storm.’

Sometimes. Birds respond to changing pressure, wind and food conditions, but flocking also belongs to migration and daily behavior. Check the forecast before assigning meteorological duties to the starlings.

‘Red sky at night, sailor’s delight.’

There is a weather principle beneath the rhyme in regions where systems commonly move west to east: sunset colors can reflect clearer air to the west. It is a clue, not a warning system. Official forecasts outrank beautiful poetry when severe weather threatens.

Frida’S Savings Corner

The September Harvest Fund

Frida believes every budget should contain four lines: necessities, savings, seasonal pleasure and catnip. Her accountant has reduced the last category, but negotiations continue.

The Fall Reset

Review the last two months of spending and circle the costs likely to change with colder weather: heating, school needs, holidays, travel, clothing and pet care. Choose one amount to save automatically each payday. A small transfer that happens is more useful than a grand intention that remains inspirational.

Shop the House First

Before groceries, decorations or fall clothing, take inventory. Build two meals around pantry food, repair one item and repurpose one decoration. The exercise is not deprivation; it is an introduction to belongings you have already paid for.

Frida’s $50 Challenge

Set aside $50 for September: $25 for winter expenses, $15 for holiday preparation and $10 for something cheerful. If $50 is not practical, divide any amount by the same proportions. Frida recommends that the cheerful portion remain under human supervision.

A Harvest Puzzle

Frida has $60. She spends $12 on pantry staples, $9 on garden supplies and $15 on a repaired coat. She puts half of what remains into winter savings. How much does she save, and how much remains for ordinary spending? The answer waits near the end.

Before October Comes

A Closing Word

September leaves us with stained fingertips, fuller shelves and the suspicion that daylight has begun slipping out early. The garden is not finished everywhere, but the year has unmistakably turned its face toward autumn.

Gather what is ready. Repair what can serve another season. Store food carefully, save a little money and step outside when the Moon rises. No household completes every task, and no respectable almanac should expect it.

May your pantry be useful, your weather manageable and your last tomato better than it has any right to be.
