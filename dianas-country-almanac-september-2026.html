Welcome to September

A month caught between two worlds.

Summer has not quite packed its bags, but autumn has already begun knocking at the door.

The garden is still offering tomatoes, peppers, squash, beans, herbs, and other summer treasures in many parts of the country, while gardeners farther north may already be thinking about the first frost. In warmer regions, there is still plenty of growing to do.

This is the month to harvest what is ready, prepare for what is coming, and enjoy the season while it is here.

Wherever you live in the United States, September is a good time to notice the little signs of change. Evenings grow darker earlier. The quality of the sunlight shifts. Some trees begin to turn. Birds prepare for journeys. And somewhere, someone is picking the first apple of the season.

The September Garden

For many gardeners, another garden is beginning.

Depending on your region, September can be a good time to plant or prepare for cool-season crops.

  • Lettuce
  • Spinach
  • Radishes
  • Carrots
  • Beets
  • Peas
  • Chard
  • Broccoli
  • Cabbage
  • Kale

But do not plant by the calendar alone. Your local climate matters.

Cooler northern regionsPrepare for frost, finish tender harvests, plant only crops suited to the remaining season, and protect what can continue.
Temperate regionsBegin cool-season crops as local temperatures allow while summer crops remain productive.
Southern and warm-winter regionsSeptember may begin a major fall growing season; follow local planting guidance.
Hot desert and tropical regionsHeat, rainfall patterns, elevation, and local seasons matter more than the calendar label “autumn.”

Use local frost dates, USDA hardiness information, seed-packet instructions, and university or county extension guidance when deciding what to plant.

September Garden To-Do List

  • Harvest ripe vegetables regularly.
  • Remove diseased plant material.
  • Clean up exhausted summer plants.
  • Add finished compost to garden beds.
  • Check irrigation and adjust only as weather requires.
  • Plant appropriate fall crops.
  • Save seeds from suitable healthy plants.
  • Mulch where appropriate.
  • Clean and organize garden tools.
Let the garden tell you when it is finished.
Do not pull up a healthy, productive plant simply because the calendar says summer is over.
The Last of Summer

A full garden can become a full pantry.

If the garden is overflowing with tomatoes, peppers, zucchini, herbs, or fruit, September is the time to decide what will be eaten now and what will be saved for later.

Tomatoes: sauce, salsa, soup, or roasting
Peppers: freezing, pickling, or sauces
Herbs: careful drying or freezing
Zucchini: bread, soup, casseroles, or freezing
Fruit: pies, cobblers, jams, or freezing
Beans: fresh meals, blanching, or tested preservation methods

For canning and shelf-stable preserving, always use a current tested recipe from a reliable source. Acidity, processing method, jar size, elevation, and timing all affect safety.

September Recipe

Easy Farmhouse Tomato Soup

Serves 4Preparation: 15 minutesCooking: about 35 minutes

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 medium onion, chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 5 cups ripe tomatoes, chopped
  • 2 cups low-sodium chicken or vegetable broth
  • 1 teaspoon salt, plus more to taste
  • ½ teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon sugar, optional
  • 1 tablespoon chopped fresh basil
  • 1 teaspoon fresh thyme leaves
  • ½ cup heavy cream, optional

Directions

  1. Heat the olive oil in a large saucepan over medium heat.
  2. Add the onion and cook for 5–7 minutes, stirring occasionally, until softened.
  3. Add the garlic and cook for 30 seconds.
  4. Add the tomatoes, broth, salt, and black pepper.
  5. Bring to a gentle simmer and cook uncovered for about 20 minutes.
  6. Stir in the basil and thyme.
  7. Carefully blend until smooth with an immersion blender. If using a countertop blender, cool slightly and follow the manufacturer’s precautions for hot liquids.
  8. Return the soup to the pan. Stir in the cream, if desired.
  9. Taste and adjust the seasoning. If the tomatoes are especially tart, add the optional sugar a little at a time.
  10. Serve warm with crusty bread.

Kitchen note: Refrigerate leftovers promptly. This is a cooking recipe, not a canning recipe; do not use it for shelf-stable storage.

The September House

A gentle household reset.

Before the holidays and colder months arrive, choose an afternoon for a little preparation.

  • Clean out the refrigerator.
  • Check and inventory the freezer.
  • Organize the pantry and rotate older foods forward.
  • Discard spoiled or unsafe food.
  • Make a grocery inventory before shopping.
  • Wash windows where needed.
  • Test smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms according to manufacturer instructions.
  • Review emergency supplies.
  • Put away summer items that will not be needed again soon.
A Little Money-Saving Wisdom
Before buying groceries, look in the pantry first. You may already have enough ingredients for dinner hiding behind the canned beans and three nearly forgotten boxes of pasta. The best grocery bargain is sometimes the food you already own.
Look Up!

The Harvest Moon

September has a beautiful gift waiting toward the end of the month. The 2026 Harvest Moon reaches full illumination on September 26. It is called the Harvest Moon because it is the full Moon closest to the September equinox. Around this time, evening moonrises occur with less delay than usual at many Northern Hemisphere latitudes, historically extending the light available during harvest season.

The September equinox occurs on September 23 at 00:05 UTC—which is September 22 at 5:05 p.m. Pacific Daylight Time and 8:05 p.m. Eastern Daylight Time. It marks the astronomical beginning of autumn in the Northern Hemisphere.

Your September Stargazing Challenge

One evening this month, turn off the television. Put down the phone. Step outside. Give your eyes time to adjust to the darkness. Then look up.

You do not need a telescope. Sometimes all you need is a chair, a clear night, and a few minutes. The sky belongs to everyone.

Weather & Old-Time Lore

Our ancestors watched, remembered, and compared.

“If the first week of September be warm, the winter will be mild.”

Is it a reliable scientific forecast? No. But it belongs to the long tradition of country weather lore.

People once watched the sky, plants, and animals because they had no weather app in a pocket. Their observations are worth preserving—even when modern meteorology gives us more dependable tools.

Throughout Diana’s Country Almanac, folklore will always be identified as folklore. Traditional sayings should never replace current forecasts or official severe-weather warnings.

September’s Little Lesson

Not everything happens at once.

The seed comes first. Then the sprout. Then the flower. Then the fruit. Then the harvest. And finally, the soil rests.

Our lives are much the same. There are seasons for beginning, working, harvesting, and letting something go.

September stands right at the doorway between them.

So harvest what is ready. Plant what belongs to the coming season. Make something delicious. Look at the Moon. And do not forget to enjoy the view.

September is only the beginning.
